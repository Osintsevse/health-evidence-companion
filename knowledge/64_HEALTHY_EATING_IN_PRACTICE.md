# Healthy eating in practice

For adults choosing everyday meals. Start with the foods a person likes, can afford and can regularly prepare. Ask whether the goal is adequate intake, convenience, a dietary preference or support for an established condition. Weight loss is only one possible goal. Pregnancy, childhood, undernutrition, disordered eating, swallowing problems and prescribed renal diets require their own guidance. See the NUTRITION clinical chapter for condition-specific questions. S188 S328 S332

## Build a few repeatable meals

Look across several usual days for vegetables and fruit, whole grains where practical, varied protein sources, and fats that come mainly from unsaturated oils, nuts or seeds. Identify a familiar meal where one change fits the budget. Dried lentils, canned beans, frozen vegetables, oats and seasonal fruit can be practical options. Compare similar products for sodium, added sugars and fibre, allowing for serving size. Plain frozen vegetables or canned produce without excess salt or added sugar can serve the same purpose as fresh produce. S188 S325

| Starting point | A practical change | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bread or porridge for breakfast | Add plain yogurt or an unsweetened fortified soy alternative and an affordable fruit. | Check the actual alternative's protein and fortification if it regularly replaces dairy. |
| Soup followed by bread | Add lentils or beans and available vegetables to the pot. | Compare salt in stock cubes and canned beans. For prescribed potassium restriction, use the person's renal plan. |
| Rice, pasta or potatoes at dinner | Keep the familiar staple. Add beans, tofu, eggs or fish and a vegetable such as cabbage or frozen peas. | Choose the protein that suits cost, preferences and the rest of the week. When appetite is low, preserve adequate energy. |

These are examples of food combinations. Portions depend on appetite, activity, nutritional needs and the rest of the day. If time or money is tight, keeping a shelf-stable or frozen option can make a planned meal possible. S188 S328 S332

## Compare a processing label with the actual food

An *ultraprocessed* classification describes features of manufacturing and formulation. It cannot tell you the fibre, sodium or protein of a particular portion, the food it replaces, or whether it supplies an otherwise missing nutrient. When choosing bread, for instance, compare whole-grain content, fibre and salt across the loaves actually available. When replacing a milk drink, check fortification. Frozen vegetables, canned beans and fortified foods warrant different questions. S188 S325 S332 S889 S890

Two studies help explain why the outcome and comparison matter.

- **Short-term weight change.** Dicken and colleagues' English UPDATE randomized crossover trial enrolled 55 adults with BMI 25 to under 40 whose habitual diet derived at least half its energy from ultraprocessed foods. Fifty contributed a primary outcome. Participants received food for two eight-week periods, with four weeks between them, and could eat to appetite. Both menus met the UK Eatwell Guide. Average weight changes were −2.06% on the minimally processed menu and −1.05% on the ultraprocessed menu. The estimated between-menu difference was −1.01 percentage points (95% CI −1.87 to −0.14). The diets differed as whole menus, monitoring at home was incomplete and a carryover effect remained possible. The study did not measure disease incidence or the effect of any single additive. S889
- **Type 2 diabetes over years.** In Thompson and colleagues' 2026 UK Biobank analysis, 124,836 adults aged 40–70 at recruitment supplied repeated dietary recalls. During follow-up spanning 8.3–10.5 years across outcomes, 3,420 developed type 2 diabetes. Comparing the highest with the lowest quartile of each healthful plant-based diet score, the adjusted diabetes hazard ratio was 0.72 (95% CI 0.65–0.79) for the score emphasizing lower-UPF foods and 0.89 (0.79–0.99) for the score emphasizing higher-UPF foods. These are separate score comparisons. They cannot be subtracted to estimate the benefit of switching an individual between the diets. Most participants contributed only two recalls, processing can be misclassified, and lifestyle differences can remain after adjustment. Other UK Biobank reports may reuse participants. Count this as one cohort when assembling evidence. S890

## Is a potassium-based salt substitute suitable for a shared meal?

Start by identifying the main salt source. When most salt comes from bread, processed meat, ready meals or sauces, comparing those products or reducing a salty condiment addresses a source that changing the salt shaker will miss. For an adult who adds table salt, WHO's 2025 guideline **conditionally** suggests replacing regular salt with a lower-sodium salt containing potassium. It addresses discretionary table salt used in cooking or at the table. It does not cover industrial reformulation, restaurant meals or salty condiments. The intended direction is less sodium overall. S188 S888

WHO excludes people with kidney impairment or other circumstances affecting potassium handling, including potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements. Its recommendation does not extend to children or pregnant people because direct evidence was insufficient. Before using one product in a shared pot, check who will eat it and whether anyone has a potassium restriction or a relevant medicine. Reducing the regular salt used, flavouring with herbs or lemon, or changing a salty ingredient provides options while checking with a clinician or pharmacist. Check the label for iodine separately where iodization matters. S328 S888 S891

## Planning a vegetarian or vegan pattern

- **Protein and energy.** Build meals around a source such as beans, peas, lentils, tofu or other soy foods. Eggs and dairy are options for people who include them. Replacing meat with vegetables alone can leave a meal short of protein and energy. Match the plan to appetite, budget and whether it can be repeated. S188 S332
- **Vitamin B12.** Verify a reliable, regular source when animal foods are excluded. Options include products explicitly fortified with B12 or an appropriate supplement. Ordinary unfortified plant foods and nutritional yeast without added B12 cannot fill this role. Use the actual product label and amount eaten. Someone with suspected deficiency or absorption problems needs individual assessment. S456
- **Iron.** Beans, lentils and fortified grains can supply iron. Combine a familiar portion with a vitamin C source, such as peppers, cabbage or fruit, to support absorption of plant iron. A menu alone cannot diagnose iron deficiency or establish a need for supplemental iron. S332
- **Calcium, vitamin D and iodine.** Check the amounts listed on the exact plant drink or yogurt. Calcium fortification does not imply that iodine, vitamin D or B12 has been added. Calcium-set tofu can contribute calcium. In its October 2026 guidance, Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment notes that unfortified plant drinks generally contribute little iodine and that some seaweeds, especially kombu, contain highly variable and sometimes excessive amounts. Verify local products and iodized-salt practices without increasing salt intake to obtain iodine. S332 S816 S891

**What the 2026 IMPACT trial adds.** In a 12-week Dutch trial, van Oppenraaij, Weijs and Verlaan reported on 108 adults, median age 66. Both arms had resistance training and food-choice counselling. The comparison was a self-selected lacto-ovo vegetarian diet with an omnivorous diet. The vegetarian arm achieved lower protein intake (estimated difference −0.33 g/kg/day, 95% CI −0.49 to −0.17). The estimated difference in appendicular muscle mass was −0.33 kg (95% CI −0.69 to 0.03). The interval crossed both zero and the prespecified noninferiority boundary of −0.25 kg. The trial therefore could not establish that the vegetarian arm retained an acceptably similar gain. The results are compatible with a smaller gain and with no difference. Intake was not equalized, so the study cannot isolate a protein-source effect at matched intake. Neither arm tested a vegan diet. Primary outcomes were food-record intake and DXA measurements at three months. The registry and publication describe the same trial. S892 S893
