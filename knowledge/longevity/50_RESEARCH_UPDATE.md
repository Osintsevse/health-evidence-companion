# Healthy longevity: what recent human studies actually measured

Targeted update checked 2026-11-25. Adults; human studies of function, clinical disease, survival and prevention. Current evidence for an individual medicine still depends on the indication and country. The review below does not yield a regimen or a personal life-expectancy estimate.

## Preserving mobility in older adults with frailty

**SPRINTT** enrolled 1,519 community-dwelling adults aged 70 or older in 11 European countries. All could initially walk 400 metres and had low muscle mass and impaired physical performance. Moderate physical activity, activity monitoring and nutritional counselling were compared with education on healthy ageing. The primary analysis was prespecified in the 1,205 participants with greater functional impairment (baseline Short Physical Performance Battery score 3–7). The mean follow-up for the trial was 26.4 months. [medical:S829]

In that stratum, 283/605 intervention participants and 316/600 controls reached the primary endpoint (46.8% versus 52.7%, hazard ratio 0.78, 95% CI 0.67–0.92). The endpoint counted the first loss of the ability to walk 400 metres independently in under 15 minutes, or death. Persistent mobility disability was a secondary endpoint with an interval spanning no difference. Recorded falls affected 80/605 versus 49/600 people (risk ratio 1.62, 95% CI 1.16–2.27). The investigators noted that more contact with the intervention group could influence adverse-event ascertainment. The exploratory, less impaired group had no clear mobility benefit. This trial supports discussing mobility and fall risks together in similar patients. It cannot isolate the contribution of the counselling or supply a longevity gain. [medical:S829]

## Aspirin and a measure of physical and mental capacity

A secondary analysis published on 2026-10-29 used 18,581 ASPREE participants aged 65–98. The authors investigated whether a composite of functional measurements predicted subsequent disability, dementia or death, and separately whether daily low-dose aspirin changed that score relative to placebo. Lower baseline capacity predicted a higher subsequent incidence of the composite outcome. Being able to identify a higher-risk group does not show that changing its score will improve survival. [medical:S830]

Over a median trial follow-up of 4.7 years, the aspirin group showed a small additional decline in the score among women in the sex-stratified analysis. The estimate in men was close to zero. The formal interaction between treatment, time and sex had p=0.06, so the analysis leaves a sex-specific treatment effect uncertain. The principal index omitted the sensory domain because of incomplete assessments. These data provide no reason to start aspirin solely to preserve functional capacity. Decisions about aspirin prescribed for established vascular disease require its own evidence and clinical review. [medical:S830]

## Nutrition: define what counts as healthy ageing

One 2025 US analysis followed two cohorts of health professionals for up to 30 years. Its definition required survival to at least 70, freedom from 11 chronic diseases and preserved physical, cognitive and mental health. Of 105,015 participants, 9,771 (9.3%) met all criteria. The highest versus lowest fifth of the Alternative Healthy Eating Index had adjusted odds of meeting this definition of 1.86 (95% CI 1.71–2.01). This odds ratio cannot be read as a percentage-point gain in an individual's chances. Diet was self-reported; long-term lifestyle differences, missing outcome data and the selected workforce limit causal interpretation and generalizability. [medical:S834]

A UK Biobank study published on 2026-11-11 examined a different clinical outcome. Among 155,413 adults without diabetes at baseline, 7,626 developed type 2 diabetes over median follow-up of 11.6 years. Compared with the lowest quartile of one EAT-Lancet diet score (Stubbendorff), the highest quartile had an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.85 (95% CI 0.78–0.92). The investigators also examined blood-based ageing indices. The order of the diet and biomarker measurements varied, and markers of glycaemia can contribute to the index and predict diabetes. The data are compatible with an association; their mediation model cannot show that diet prevents diabetes by slowing ageing, or that it increases lifespan. [medical:S831]

## Supplements and patient-reported function

A prespecified secondary analysis of COSMOS, published on 2026-11-18, compared a daily multivitamin–mineral supplement with placebo within a factorial trial of 21,442 US adults (men aged at least 60 and women aged at least 65). Across three years, the reported between-group differences were 0.44 points for symptom burden (95% CI 0.13–0.74) and 0.33 points for a cardiovascular clinical summary score (0.09–0.57). The physical ability score showed no statistically significant overall difference. These are small changes on questionnaire scales. The report does not establish a meaningful extension of independent life, a mortality benefit, or benefit from an individual ingredient. Treatment of a documented deficiency is a separate question. Only the abstract and public funding information were inspected; completeness of follow-up and harms could not be appraised independently here. [medical:S832]

## A newly registered trial has no efficacy result

The VITAL-H registry record was first posted 2026-11-06. At the 2026-11-25 check, the sponsor listed the trial as not yet recruiting and no results had been posted. The plan compares semaglutide, dapagliflozin and placebo in an estimated 1,200 community-dwelling adults aged 60–75; diabetes and selected major conditions are exclusions. The primary outcome is change in a lower-extremity performance battery over approximately 36 months. Recruitment and completion dates were estimates. A randomized design would support a comparison after results become available; a registration alone supplies no treatment effect or safety estimate. Recheck the dated record before discussing status. [medical:S833]
