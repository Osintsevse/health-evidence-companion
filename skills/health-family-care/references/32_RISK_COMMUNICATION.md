# Explain adverse effects and probabilities

Read the exact product/formulation/market label and distinguish trial observations from post-marketing reports. A list of possible effects is not a prediction that this person will experience all of them. Frequency, severity, reversibility and urgency are separate dimensions.

## Translate label terms

The inspected EMA frequency convention uses: very common at least 10%; common at least 1% but below 10%; uncommon at least 0.1% but below 1%; rare at least 0.01% but below 0.1%; very rare below 0.01%; not known when the available data cannot estimate frequency. Confirm the convention in the actual local label. These are categories, not exact patient-specific probabilities. [S212]

“Uncommon” can be restated as at least 1 but fewer than 10 people per 1,000 in the stated evidence context. “Not known” means no reliable frequency estimate, not zero risk and not necessarily a very rare event. Do not manufacture a percentage from spontaneous reporting counts or add several listed percentages to estimate the chance of any side effect.

## Make comparisons interpretable

Use the same denominator and time horizon for treatment and comparator. For a wholly synthetic example, 20 versus 10 events per 1,000 over a year gives 10 additional events per 1,000 (one percentage point) and twice the relative risk. An applicable causal comparison would imply an NNH of 100 over that year. Without a comparator, baseline and timeframe, a label percentage cannot supply that attributable increase. This arithmetic is not a real medicine estimate.

Explain confidence intervals and population differences without presenting ranges as individual guarantees. Distinguish odds ratios, risk ratios and hazard ratios. Do not convert them interchangeably or imply that a risk reduction is a percentage of extra lifetime. NICE supports consistent denominators, absolute risk and natural-frequency communication. [S194]

## Connect the risk to an action

Prioritize serious time-sensitive symptoms and the source-backed response, then common manageable effects and monitoring. Balance the treatment's intended benefit, alternative options and the patient's burden/preferences. Do not advise independent discontinuation because an effect appears in a list; urgent adverse reactions follow the appropriate emergency guidance. Explain what is uncertain and what information a prescriber or pharmacist needs.

For suspected adverse effects, establish timing, exact exposure, other medicines and alternative explanations. Temporal association alone does not prove causality. Ask the person to describe the comparison in their own words when confusion would change a decision. See module 29 for shared decisions and module 12 for interactions.
