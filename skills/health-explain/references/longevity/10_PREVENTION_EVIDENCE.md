# Prevention evidence for healthy longevity

Checked 2026-10-18. Original general educational synthesis; substantive maintainer and suitable clinical review remain pending. This is a bounded study packet, not a systematic review or an individually prescribed prevention programme. Patient-relevant outcomes, risk-factor measurements and recommendations are different evidence objects.

## medical:S767 — Smoking cessation and cigarette mortality associations

Thomson et al., *JAMA Network Open* 2022;5:e2231480. [Article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2797597).

US prospective NHIS cohort: 551,388 adults recruited in 1997–2018, mortality linkage through 2019; 74,870 deaths at ages 25–89. Baseline smoking was self-reported. Compared with never smoking, adjusted all-cause mortality rate ratios were 2.80 (95% CI 2.73–2.88) for current smoking and 1.37 (1.34–1.40) for former smoking. Quitting before 35: 1.03 (0.99–1.07); at 35–44: 1.21 (1.17–1.26); at 45–54: 1.47 (1.42–1.53); at 55–64: 1.74 (1.68–1.80), each versus never smoking.

These are adjusted observational associations, not randomized cessation-treatment effects. Illness-driven quitting, residual confounding and changes after baseline remain concerns. A near-null estimate is not proof of identical individual risk. No absolute cessation benefit or added life-years is estimated here. Funded by the American Cancer Society; outside-work disclosures reported. Selected methods, results and limitations read; supplements not appraised. Article-specific CC-BY.

## medical:S768 — Smoking cessation: practical tobacco support

WHO's [2024 guideline announcement](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-07-2024-who-releases-first-ever-clinical-treatment-guideline-for-tobacco-cessation-in-adults) summarizes its [adult guideline](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240096431). Global scope; published 2024-07-02.

Offer routine brief advice and access to more intensive individual, group or telephone counselling. WHO recommends varenicline, nicotine replacement, bupropion and cytisine as effective cessation options, with behavioural support and pharmacotherapy combined when suitable. Text messages, apps and online support can supplement care.

A practical discussion can identify the tobacco product, readiness, triggers and previous attempts, choose an attainable quit plan, arrange support and follow-up, and discuss suitable licensed medication with a clinician or pharmacist. Lapses should prompt reassessment and another attempt. Drug choice requires current local labels, contraindications and individual circumstances; this packet supplies no dosing or pregnancy regimen.

Outcome: successful cessation, not a quantified radical-longevity effect. The announcement and overview were read; the complete guideline, evidence tables and medicine-specific harms were not appraised. WHO copyright/noncommercial conditions and third-party exceptions remain; no source table or algorithm reproduced.

## medical:S769 — Aerobic and strengthening activity: mortality association

Zhao et al., *BMJ* 2020;370:m2031. [Article](https://www.bmj.com/content/370/bmj.m2031).

Prospective US NHIS cohort, 479,856 adults aged ≥18; self-reported leisure activity, mortality linkage through 2015. Median follow-up 8.75 years; 59,819 deaths. Against insufficient aerobic and strengthening activity, fully adjusted all-cause mortality HRs were 0.89 (95% CI 0.85–0.94) for strengthening alone, 0.71 (0.69–0.72) for aerobic alone and 0.60 (0.57–0.62) for both recommended levels. These groups contained 21,428, 113,851 and 76,384 participants; reference n=268,193.

Associations support activity recommendations but do not identify each person's causal benefit. Self-report, baseline disease severity, reverse causality and residual confounding limit inference. No randomized injury estimate or absolute treatment benefit established. Do not convert HR 0.60 into “40% more years”. Academic/foundation support; disclosures reported.

Selected indexed full-text methods, results, limitations and licence inspected; direct page/PDF access intermittently failed. CC BY-NC 4.0; original factual summary, no reproduced figure/table; noncommercial source conditions retained.

## medical:S770 — Activity recommendations and function

[WHO Europe physical activity guidance](https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/physical-activity), 2021-09-01, reflects the 2020 global guideline; recommendation summary checked 2026-10-18.

Adults generally should accumulate 150–300 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity or 75–150 minutes vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination, plus strengthening of major muscle groups on ≥2 days/week. Older adults should also include varied balance and strength activity on ≥3 days/week to support function and prevent falls. Chronic illness, disability and pregnancy require the applicable population-specific guidance.

Start from current capacity and build a sustainable routine; the recommendation does not require an immediate leap to the full amount. A routine may combine walking/cycling with suitable strengthening, and replace some prolonged sitting with movement. These recommendations concern health and function; they do not promise a fixed survival gain. The fact sheet was read, not every underlying guideline evidence table. WHO copyright/noncommercial and third-party conditions retained; no source exercise protocol reproduced.

## medical:S771 — Blood-pressure lowering: randomized cardiovascular outcomes

BPLTTC, *Lancet* 2021;397:1625–1636. [Licensed full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8102467/).

Individual-participant analysis of 48 randomized trials, n=344,716, comparing medications with placebo/other classes or more versus less intensive treatment. Mean age approximately 65; median follow-up 4.15 years. Primary outcome combined fatal/nonfatal stroke, myocardial infarction/ischaemic heart disease, and fatal or hospitalized heart failure; 42,324 participants experienced it. Per standardized 5-mmHg systolic reduction, HR was 0.91 (95% CI 0.89–0.94) without previous CVD and 0.89 (0.86–0.92) with previous CVD.

This is a clinical-event result, not evidence of reversing biological ageing. It does not specify one optimal target or justify medication for every healthy person. Harms were explicitly not investigated; decisions need baseline risk, tolerability and current local guidance. Funding: British Heart Foundation, NIHR and Oxford Martin School; no funder analytic role reported. Selected methods, results, limitations and rights read; appendix not appraised. CC BY 4.0. Avoid transferring trial absolute rates directly to a different population.

## medical:S772 — Statins: absolute and relative primary-prevention effects

Chou et al., USPSTF [2022 evidence report](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/final-evidence-summary/statin-use-in-adults-preventive-medication); *JAMA* 328:754–771.

Review included 22 statin-versus-placebo/no-statin trials, n=90,624, follow-up 6 months–6 years. All-cause mortality pool: 18 trials, n=85,816; RR 0.92 (95% CI 0.87–0.98), absolute risk difference −0.35 percentage points (−0.57 to −0.14), across differing trial horizons. This is not a fixed-duration personal NNT or a biological-age effect.

Serious adverse events: RR 0.97 (0.93–1.01); diabetes overall: RR 1.04 (0.92–1.19), with uncertainty and a high-intensity-trial signal. The estimates do not mean every individual is free of adverse effects. Evidence above age 75 was sparse; trials enrolled people with CVD risk factors. Funding: AHRQ; report-specific disclosures retained at source. Selected abstract, methods, results and limitations read. USPSTF reuse notice checked; brief original factual synthesis, no copied report/table and no agency endorsement. No open licence asserted for the JAMA work.

## medical:S773 — Statin recommendations require a risk discussion

[USPSTF final recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/statin-use-in-adults-preventive-medication), 2022-08-23; US primary prevention.

For adults 40–75 with ≥1 CVD risk factor and estimated 10-year CVD risk ≥10%, USPSTF recommends prescribing a statin after discussion (grade B). At 7.5%–<10%, selectively offer it according to preferences (grade C). Evidence is insufficient for initiating primary-prevention statins at ≥76. These criteria exclude established CVD, LDL cholesterol >190 mg/dL and known familial hypercholesterolaemia, which need other pathways.

Action: obtain appropriate lipids and confirmed blood-pressure information, assess smoking/diabetes and overall cardiovascular risk, then discuss benefits, harms and options with a clinician. A US threshold is not an automatic rule for another country or an “anti-ageing” indication. Recommendation summary and scope read; not a completed review of European guidance. USPSTF copyright/reuse conditions retained. This original educational synthesis has no USPSTF, AHRQ or HHS endorsement; no calculator or recommendation table reproduced.

## medical:S774 — Mediterranean-pattern adherence: confounding matters

Ahmad et al., *JAMA Network Open* 2024;7:e2414322. [Article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2819335).

Prospective cohort of 25,315 initially healthy US female health professionals, mean baseline age 54.6; mean follow-up 24.7 years; 3,879 deaths. Higher versus lower diet-score adherence had all-cause mortality HR 0.77 (95% CI 0.70–0.84) after age, randomized original-trial assignment and energy adjustment. The model additionally accounting for lifestyle and menopausal factors attenuated the association to 0.89 (0.82–0.98).

Diet was not randomized. Baseline food questionnaires, predominantly White educated women, residual confounding and uncertain mediator interpretation limit generalization. The historical diet score includes alcohol; this does not establish that starting alcohol is beneficial. No causal absolute benefit or life-years calculated. NIH support and several author industry/patent disclosures reported.

Selected methods, results including model definitions, limitations and rights read; supplements not appraised. CC-BY. Both models must accompany quantitative teaching: the headline “23% lower mortality” alone conceals important adjustment sensitivity.

## medical:S188 — A practical dietary foundation

[WHO Healthy diet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet), updated 2026-01-26; global public-health guidance.

For adults, build variety around vegetables, fruit, pulses and whole grains, with nutritional adequacy, balance and moderation. For people older than 10, WHO gives ≥400 g/day fruit/vegetables and ≥25 g/day naturally occurring dietary fibre. Free sugars should remain <10% of energy, with further reduction potentially useful; prefer unsaturated fats and avoid industrial trans fats. The selected facts do not establish a universal restrictive diet, supplement stack or fixed calorie prescription.

Action: make durable substitutions that fit available foods and medical needs; assess nutrition and physical function when reducing weight. Food allergy, malnutrition, frailty, kidney disease and other conditions can require different choices. The fact sheet's selected adult sections were read; no complete appraisal of every underpinning study. This is guidance, not an individual mortality estimate. WHO copyright/noncommercial and third-party conditions retained; no source menu, table, image or algorithm reproduced.

## medical:S775 — Alcohol: no established mortality protection from low intake

Zhao et al., *JAMA Network Open* 2023;6:e236185. [Corrected article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2802963).

Meta-analysis of 107 observational cohorts: 4,838,825 participants and 425,564 deaths; varying follow-up. Against lifetime nondrinking, fully adjusted all-cause mortality RR for 1.3–<25 g ethanol/day was 0.93 (95% CI 0.85–1.01); for 45–<65 g/day, 1.19 (1.07–1.32), and ≥65 g/day, 1.35 (1.23–1.47).

The low-volume interval includes no association and does not prove either equivalence or benefit. Self-report, former-drinker misclassification, study-level adjustment and residual confounding remain limitations. An all-cause null interval cannot establish a cancer-safe dose. No universal absolute harm estimate or causal longevity gain is supplied. Funded partly through CCSA/Health Canada; author disclosures reported. Selected methods/results/limitations and correction notice read; individual cohort studies not independently appraised. Corrected 2023-05-09 (figure/supplement). CC-BY. Do not start drinking for longevity on this evidence.

## medical:S207 — Alcohol prevention guidance

[WHO Alcohol fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/alcohol), updated 2024-06-28; global public-health scope.

Alcohol carries health risks even at low intake; much harm arises from heavy continuous or episodic consumption. Avoid treating a daily drink as a preventive medicine. For someone who drinks, reducing amount and avoiding heavy episodes are reasonable risk-reduction goals; review medication interactions and personal contraindications separately. Dependence requires an appropriate clinical pathway rather than a generic self-directed abrupt-withdrawal instruction.

WHO's global alcohol-attributable death estimates are population burden estimates; they cannot determine an individual's probability of death. No abstinence-treatment mortality effect or personal “safe allowance” is calculated here. Selected health-risk sections read, not the complete global report or alcohol-use-disorder treatment evidence. WHO copyright/noncommercial and third-party conditions retained; original factual synthesis, no chart or tool copied.

## medical:S776 — Sleep: adequate opportunity and appropriate evaluation

[NHLBI, How Much Sleep Is Enough?](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep/how-much-sleep), updated 2022-03-24, checked 2026-10-18; US public education.

Most adults should allow approximately 7–9 hours/night. Longer sleep can be appropriate for young adults, recovery from sleep deprivation or illness; the range is not a rigid diagnostic test. Persistent too little/too much sleep warrants discussing the problem with a clinician.

Action: protect sufficient sleep opportunity and a sustainable routine, and seek evaluation when persistent sleep difficulty or daytime impairment remains. This source supplies no randomized mortality estimate for sleeping a specified number of hours. It does not justify sedative treatment to achieve a number or establish a causal “years gained” estimate.

The complete short adult-duration section and NHLBI reuse notice were read. Source: National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute; National Institutes of Health; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. Federal public-domain text category with third-party/logo exceptions; no endorsement implied.

## Applying the packet without overstating it

Prioritize cessation support, sustainable activity and nutrition, adequate sleep, and identification/treatment of established cardiovascular risks. Use vaccination and screening modules with age, jurisdiction and risk-specific guidance; their complete evidence is outside this packet. Separate a prevention discussion from experimental geroscience.

Do not add, multiply or rank these effect estimates as if they were interchangeable. They concern different populations, comparators, models and time horizons. The same behaviour may influence several endpoints and studies may share participants. A hazard ratio is not an absolute risk, expected life-years or chance of reaching a future rejuvenation technology. Read current local guidance and medicine labels before an individual treatment choice.

Remaining study: causal dietary mortality trials with usable rights; randomized exercise/function and falls outcomes; current local BP/lipid guidance; diabetes prevention and long-term clinical outcomes; vaccination, cancer screening, injury prevention and social determinants. Rights-limited PREDIMED/SPRINT works remain bibliographic pointers under source-use policy; they are not active evidence in this packet.
