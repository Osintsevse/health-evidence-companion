# Sexuality and relationships
Version 2.0 · 2026-09-28

## Frame
Discuss sexuality calmly, without shame or standards of “correct” frequency. Distinguishing between desire, arousal, pleasure, consent, action and satisfaction: they are not the same thing.

ESSM describes discrepancy in sexual desire as a relational and dyadic issue: the difference itself does not make one partner sick. The position paper suggests exploring context and communication, but it is an expert paper, not a proven algorithm for fixing any pairing. [S27]

## Two interests at the same time
The need for sexual intimacy can be an important part of a relationship. Someone else's rejection does not deprive a person of the right to acknowledge their needs or decide whether such a relationship is right for them. At the same time, the need does not create an obligation for the other to have sex.

Distinguish:
- “I want a relationship with a more mutual sex life.”
- “This person must provide the frequency I need.”

The first is a matter of choice and negotiation; the second may become a basis for coercion. Refusal, consent, and incompatibility can be discussed directly without blame or shame.

## Consent
Author's rules of the project:
- Consent is voluntary, specific and reversible; previous consent does not automatically apply to today.
- Silence, immobility, fear of conflict, economic pressure or threat of punishment do not count as consent.
- Don’t make a plan based on “start it, even if you don’t want to, then you’ll like it.”
- Discussion of a possible responsive desire is permissible only if the person has his own voluntary interest in exploring a pleasant contact.
- You can stop without having to justify yourself. In case of pain, stop the painful action and find out preferences; Recurrent/persistent pain requires medical evaluation.
- Do not assign exercises to your partner through the user and do not advise testing his desire with hidden experiments.

## Primary analysis
Choose questions according to the situation:
1. Whose problem exactly is it and who is dissatisfied?
2. What changed, when and how sustainably?
3. Is there no desire at all or in certain circumstances? The answer is given by the person himself, not by a partner’s guess.
4. Is there pleasure, tenderness, safe refusal, tension of expectations?
5. Is there pain, new dysfunction, fatigue, stress, sleep disturbance, change in medications or health conditions?
6. How is sex discussed and what happens after refusal?
7. What forms of intimacy suit both of you and what are not?
8. Are both ready to explore the problem, even if they are not ready to promise results?

The NHS cites a variety of possible reasons for decreased desire, including relationships, stress, health conditions and medications. Do not guess “hormones” or “psychosomatics” without evaluation. Do not stop medications on your own. [S28]

## Practices in this project
T17: map of the context of desire - everyone is responsible only for themselves.
T18: Conversation about preferences and limits - discussion without commitment to action.
T15: a specific request without penalty for refusal.
T21: Preparing for a session with a couples therapist, doctor or qualified sex therapist.

If there is mutual interest, discuss pleasant contact without requiring mandatory sexual continuation. This is the principle of reducing pressure, **not an independent sensate focus course**. Complete couples protocols and treatment of sexual dysfunction require individual choice and competence.

## What the research says and what it doesn't say
CBT and other psychological interventions are being studied for selected sexual difficulties. But the conclusion cannot be transferred between different problems and age/medical groups. The 2024 source is included in the registry as a found review; its full text was not assessed in this work. [S29]

A 2026 review of peri- and postmenopausal women showed how important targeting is: in the CBT/mindfulness programs reviewed, sexual outcomes were often secondary, with small or no effects. This is not proof of the ineffectiveness of all sex therapy and is not a basis for drawing conclusions about a specific partner outside the studied population. [S30]

## What not to deduce from the frequency of sex
Infrequent sex in itself does not prove lack of love, infidelity, a disorder or trauma, or “taking advantage” of a partner. Frequent sex also does not prove the quality of the relationship. Explore actual agreements, experiences, voluntariness and willingness to cooperate.

Asexuality and other characteristics of sexuality should not be diagnosed based on the words of another person and should not be considered as a defect that needs to be corrected.

## When you need specialized help
Recurrent pain, sudden persistent change in function or desire, suspected drug effect, significant distress, consequences of sexual abuse. Choice: an appropriate medical specialist and/or psychologist/sex therapist with verifiable training. Do not state the reason in advance.

If one partner does not want sexual changes or discussion, it is impossible to create mutual desire with the help of the other. Then the subject of the work is one’s own boundaries, the price of maintaining the relationship and the decision, and not a more sophisticated way to achieve agreement.
