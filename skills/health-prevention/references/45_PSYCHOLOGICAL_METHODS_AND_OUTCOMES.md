## PS1 — Fit the evidence and method to the actual problem

**Scope:** CBT, ACT and Wells-style metacognitive therapy (MCT) in specified adult contexts; MCT is different from metacognitive training.

Evidence-based practice integrates relevant research, clinician expertise and the person's characteristics, culture and preferences. Consider the problem, functioning, context, goals, clinician skill, available format and need for further assessment. A familiar technique name or plausible mechanism does not establish that the whole intervention fits this person. [psychology:S01, psychology:S105]

WHO mhGAP 2023 ANX2 makes a strong recommendation, with moderate-certainty evidence, for brief structured interventions based on CBT principles for adults with GAD and/or panic disorder. ANX3 separately makes a conditional recommendation with low-certainty evidence about delivery formats and preferences/resources. This is not a universal ranking of named therapies or evidence that an arbitrary app/chat delivers the evaluated care. Preserve the guideline's intended non-specialist-care setting and training/supervision needs. [psychology:S02]

Two original trials illustrate narrower comparisons. A 2021 follow-up report of one 82-person adult-depression ACT/CBT trial found no statistically significant between-method difference; this is not proof of equivalence, and the report is not an independent new trial. A small three-arm GAD trial favoured its studied MCT protocol over CBT on worry/recovery outcomes; this does not establish universal MCT superiority. Samples, protocols, follow-up and outcomes differ. [psychology:S106, psychology:S107]

**Original fictional example:** an adult asks which of three methods is best for persistent worry. Clarify the question and relevant clinical context without diagnosing. Explain that condition-specific evidence, preferences, access and clinician discussion matter. A nonsignificant comparison cannot settle equivalence; a favourable trial cannot rank methods for every condition.

## PS2 — Review symptoms, daily life, burden and unwanted effects separately

**Scope:** questions for reviewing psychological care; no diagnostic or outcome-measure administration.

A lower symptom score can coexist with persistent difficulty at work, in relationships or in valued activities. Agree on relevant goals and review the person's account, function, acceptability and burden, including worsening or new problems. Progress review should inform discussion with the clinician and possible revision of the plan. A stuck point can involve formulation, therapist/intervention fit, intensity or external health/context factors; it is not automatically a personal failure. [psychology:S01, psychology:S105]

A standardized score can support observation when its use fits the population and purpose. Separate measurement error, reliable change, statistical significance and importance to the person; they are different questions. A score is not itself a diagnosis. A change after treatment does not alone prove causation or improvement in every outcome. The general measurement note F4 provides further limits. [medical:S682, psychology:S106, psychology:S107]

The inspected studies use different symptom, quality-of-life and diagnostic/reliable-change outcomes. Their outcome sets do not establish a universal CBT/ACT/MCT monitoring instrument or comprehensive capture of unwanted effects. Do not interpret an absence of reported harm as proof of no harm; ask about it directly. The separately discovered adverse-event review remains abstract-only and is not used here for a risk estimate. [psychology:S106, psychology:S107]

**Original fictional example:** a person's score improves while daily functioning is unchanged and practice has become distressing. Acknowledge all three observations and encourage discussion of benefit, burden and safety with the clinician. Do not declare recovery, failure or a required treatment change from the score alone. Immediate safety concerns take priority over routine tracking.
