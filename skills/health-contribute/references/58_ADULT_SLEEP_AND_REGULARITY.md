# Adult sleep timing, insomnia and shift work

For adults who want a steadier schedule, the first question is what happens when there is enough time to sleep. A person who regularly stays up to finish work, a person who sleeps well only on a later schedule, and a person who struggles to sleep even on free days need different help. Ask when sleep is possible on workdays and free days, what actually happens, and how the person feels during the day. A pattern can guide assessment. Timing alone cannot diagnose a sleep disorder. [S648, S672, S674, S821]

## Building a regular schedule

1. **Protect enough time for sleep.** For someone on a daytime schedule, choose a realistic time to get up on workdays and days off. Work backwards to make room for sleep and winding down. NHLBI gives 7–9 hours of actual sleep as a general adult range. Recovery from sleep loss or illness can increase the need. If the schedule allows only five hours, the first change is to free up time. Moving the alarm earlier would compound the shortage. [S648, S776]
2. **Change one practical obstacle.** For example, move the last work call earlier, replace a late caffeinated drink, or arrange quiet in a shared room. A quiet, dark, comfortably cool room and some outdoor time during the day may help. For workdays and days off, NHLBI offers roughly an hour of schedule variation as a practical aim. There is no established minute at which an otherwise healthy bedtime becomes dangerous. [S648, S823]
3. **Observe what changes.** For one to two weeks, keep a simple diary of approximate bed and wake times, estimated sleep, naps and daytime sleepiness. Add the timing of caffeine, alcohol or shifts when relevant. Compare workdays with free days. Has the person gained time asleep? Is it easier to stay awake at work? Did a family or work obligation make the plan impractical? Use the answers to adjust the plan. A diary also helps a clinician assess persisting symptoms. [S648, S821]

The sequence above is a practical application of NHLBI guidance. It has not been tested as a distinct three-step intervention. People with insomnia should have access to treatment even if they cannot control every aspect of their schedule.

## When routine changes are insufficient

Difficulty falling or staying asleep, despite adequate opportunity, together with daytime impairment deserves clinical attention. Three or more affected nights per week for at least three months is a chronicity criterion. There is no need to wait three months when functioning or safety is affected. Ask about loud snoring, gasping, witnessed pauses or unintended dozing, since sleep apnea can coexist with insomnia. Difficulty sleeping at a required hour with easier sleep on free days can also warrant assessment of circadian timing. For diagnostic pathways, see the [adult sleep assessment](43_EVIDENCE_SLEEP_AGEING_REPRODUCTIVE.md). [S657, S672, S673, S674, S821]

Multicomponent cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia. It addresses beliefs and behaviors that can perpetuate insomnia. A handout about sleep habits does not deliver this treatment. Discuss access to trained care or an evidence-based structured program. Deliberate restriction of time in bed can initially increase sleepiness and needs particular care with safety-sensitive work, excessive sleepiness, bipolar illness or poorly controlled seizures. See the [insomnia treatment evidence](42_STUDIED_QUESTIONS.md). [S649, S657]

**Concurrent medication and CBT-I.** The 2026 AASM guideline examined starting both together. It favored the combination over medication alone and generally favored CBT-I alone over starting both. Both judgments were conditional and based on low-certainty evidence. The trade-off may differ when a patient prioritizes increasing sleep time early in treatment. The evidence covers a limited range of medications. Changing or stopping an existing prescription and adding therapy after an inadequate response are separate clinical decisions. [S826]

## Adapting to night shifts

Ask whether nights are permanent or rotate, when the person can sleep between shifts, and whether the commute is safe. Protect the daytime sleep period from interruptions, noise and light. If caffeine is used, taking it earlier in the shift leaves more time before the intended sleep period. A discussion with the employer about rotations or recovery time can address constraints outside the worker's control. [S648, S822]

In adults with **shift work disorder** who are excessively sleepy, the October 2026 AASM guideline conditionally supports a pre-shift nap. Evidence certainty is very low, and alertness can be impaired immediately after waking. Leave time to recover before safety-sensitive activity. The guideline considers changing the body clock to match nights an unrealistic goal with frequently rotating shifts. Persistent sleepiness or difficulty sleeping despite protected time merits occupational-health or sleep assessment. [S648, S822]

If someone is struggling to stay awake at the wheel, pull over as soon as it is safe. If drowsy before setting out, postpone the trip or arrange another ride. Caffeine and confidence about being awake cannot establish fitness to drive. [S658, S828]

## What sleep research can establish

- **Regular timing and mortality.** Cribb and colleagues estimated sleep–wake regularity from one week of wrist movement in 88,975 UK Biobank participants, average age 62. Over a median 7.1 years there were 3,010 deaths. Comparing the fifth percentile of regularity with the median, the adjusted all-cause mortality hazard ratio was 1.53 (95% CI 1.41–1.66). The association was stronger early in follow-up. Illness can disrupt timing, and the algorithm missed naps outside the main sleep period. A hazard ratio from this cohort cannot give an individual the years of life gained by following a schedule. [S823]
- **What a watch score measures.** A comparison by Czeisler and colleagues calculated regularity two ways from the same accelerometer data in 73,794 adults. The choice of calculator changed scores and estimates of associations with health outcomes. Ask which algorithm and version generated a number before comparing it with a study. A consumer score has no clinical target established by these studies. The authors' abstract and significance statement were assessed for this comparison. [S827]
- **Blood pressure after a schedule change.** Thosar and colleagues observed 11 middle-aged adults with hypertension before and after two weeks of a more consistent bedtime. Ten had complete 24-hour blood pressure data. Their mean 24-hour systolic and diastolic pressures fell by about 4 and 3 mmHg, respectively. Without a concurrent control group, repeat measurement, time and other changes remain possible explanations. No cardiovascular event or survival benefit was measured. This pilot cannot justify reducing prescribed treatment. [S824]
- **Caffeine amount and timing.** In Gardiner and colleagues' randomized crossover trial, 23 young men who habitually consumed caffeine received single doses or placebo. Four hours before bedtime, 400 mg shortened objectively measured sleep by an estimated 51 minutes versus placebo. The trial detected no significant sleep change with 100 mg at its tested times, with the latest dose four hours before bed. The result cannot set a universal cutoff for other people, repeated servings or higher-risk groups. Timing caffeine earlier or using less is a reasonable change to try when it seems to interfere with sleep. [S648, S825]

**Evidence scope.** A targeted search on 25 November 2026 covered adult insomnia, schedules, caffeine, shift work, actigraphy and cardiometabolic outcomes. The source register records reading depth for each study. A new randomized trial with long-term patient-important outcomes, an update to the sleep guidelines or validation of a specific device would warrant a fresh review.
