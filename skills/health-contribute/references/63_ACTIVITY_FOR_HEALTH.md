# Physical activity for health and everyday function

For an adult getting started, choose an activity that fits their abilities, is easy to repeat, and supports something they want to do. Walking to the shops, cycling, dancing, wheelchair propulsion and recreational sport can all contribute. The same activity can be easy for one person and strenuous for another. Ask about current activity, symptoms, access and the person's goal before suggesting a change. [S190, S879, S881]

## What a balanced week includes

| Purpose | General adult guidance | What it can look like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Endurance | WHO recommends 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week, 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix. | At a moderate effort conversation is usually possible, while singing is difficult. At vigorous effort a person can usually say only a few words between breaths. A brisk walk, a swim or a dance session may fit, depending on the person's fitness. [S190, S770, S879] |
| Strength | Work the major muscle groups at moderate or greater effort on at least two days per week. | Options include resistance bands, weights and body-weight movements suited to the person's ability. Walking alone may leave upper-body and other muscle groups unchallenged. [S190, S880, S881] |
| Balance and everyday function in older adults | From age 65, WHO also recommends varied activity emphasizing balance and strength on three or more days per week. These activities can occur on the same days as other movement. | Supported or adapted balance practice and suitable tai chi are examples. A person with recurrent falls or difficulty standing can ask a physiotherapist to choose safe challenges in the setting where they will practise. [S190, S770, S880, S885] |

**When activity has been low.** For an adult who feels well and can walk comfortably, an example starting point is a few minutes of walking after lunch on several days. Someone who uses a wheelchair might prefer a short accessible route or a handcycle. Think about whether the activity fits the day, how it feels, and how the person recovers. If it is comfortable and repeatable, add a little time or another day before trying a much harder effort. The starting duration can be shorter. Weekly reference amounts are a longer-term goal. [S190, S881, S886]

**When much of the day is spent sitting.** One practical choice is to replace part of a sedentary break with a walk or adapted movement. Even light movement counts toward reducing sedentary time. WHO found insufficient evidence to set a universal maximum for hours sitting or a particular frequency or length of breaks. Short periods of activity can contribute to weekly totals. [S190]

## When the approach needs to change

- **Chronic conditions or disability.** For people without contraindications, WHO generally does not require a medical clearance before light or moderate activity comparable to daily tasks or brisk walking. Established heart disease, complications, new symptoms, an operation or a known restriction can change what is appropriate. A clinician or rehabilitation professional can match activities to the actual limitation and the person's treatment plan. Accessible routes, equipment, supported sessions or transport may determine what is realistic. [S190, S881, S886]
- **Symptoms during activity.** Stop for new chest discomfort, dizziness or unusual breathlessness. Call local emergency services for persistent or spreading chest pain, chest pain with sweating or breathlessness, severe difficulty breathing, or fainting during exercise. Recurrent symptoms that settle still need medical assessment before resuming the provoking activity. [S460, S466, S469, S886]

### Exercise that makes symptoms worse the next day

In some people with long COVID or myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), even modest physical or mental effort can trigger a marked worsening 12–48 hours later that lasts days or weeks. Ask what happens the next day after ordinary activities. With this pattern, pause automatic increases and seek clinical assessment. In ME/CFS, pacing means planning activity and rest around the individual's current limits, including chores and mental demands. A good day is a poor reason to make up missed sessions. The goal is to retain tolerable activity and avoid repeated setbacks. [S882, S883]

## What the research measured

**Daily movement and mortality.** In a German cohort published in November 2026, 65,028 adults aged 19–74 wore a hip accelerometer for a baseline assessment. Over a median eight years, 1,634 participants died. With the 25th percentile of average movement volume as the reference, the adjusted hazard ratio was 0.87 (95% CI 0.83–0.90) at the median and 0.73 (0.67–0.80) at the 75th percentile. These are comparisons between people. Illness can reduce movement, behavior can change after measurement, and hip devices capture cycling and upper-body activity imperfectly. The results cannot identify how many years a particular workout would add. [S884]

**Can a program prevent falls?** In an Australian randomized trial, 317 adults aged 70 or older who lived at home and had a recent history of recurrent or injurious falls were assigned to one of three programs. At one year, participants taught to embed tailored balance and strength activities in everyday tasks had 1.66 falls per person-year, compared with 2.28 in the gentle-exercise control group. The incidence rate ratio was 0.69 (95% CI 0.48–0.99). This counts recurrent events. Two participants in the active programs attributed serious injuries to exercise. The selection criteria and individualized professional instruction matter when applying the result. [S885]

The BEST at Home trial asked a different question. It randomized 617 community-dwelling adults aged at least 65 to workshops teaching home exercises for lower-body strength and balance or upper-body exercises. The falls analysis included 579 people over 12 months. Its rate ratio of 0.91 (95% CI 0.64–1.29) left the size and direction of the difference uncertain. Improvements on gait or fear-of-falling measures do not establish a reduction in actual falls. These trials differed in participants, delivery and control activity, so they cannot establish which delivery format is superior. [S887]

**Evidence scope.** General adult guidance and selected clinical outcomes were checked on 25 November 2026. These studies address habitual movement and falls in defined populations. A return to competition after injury, a disease-specific exercise prescription, or rehabilitation for post-exertional symptoms requires a separate assessment.
