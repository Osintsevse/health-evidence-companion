# Tasks, attention and personal experiments

Reviewed 2026-10-29. Optional support for adults dealing with everyday task overload, unclear next steps and competing commitments. These are original project adaptations informed by Maxim Dorofeev's two productivity books, not a psychotherapy protocol or a validated productivity programme. Use the [routing guide](16_AUTO_ROUTING.md) and [outcome review](32_OUTCOMES_AND_REVIEW.md).

## Sources, comparison and reading limits

The inspected **Jedi Techniques** edition is the Russian 2017 book, ISBN 978-5-00100-685-5 [S113]. Its useful starting point is an external system that separates collecting, deciding and doing. **The Jedi's Path**, inspected in its corrected and expanded third Russian edition, 2021, ISBN 978-5-00169-383-3 [S114], adds a stronger emphasis on personal experiments, optional observation tools and adapting commitments to capacity. Both discuss reviewing tasks; the later book develops rather than invalidates that earlier foundation. ## Choose the point of difficulty

Respond to the person's concern before offering a tool. Clarify only what changes the next step: is the difficulty remembering, knowing where to start, deciding what matters, having enough capacity, or dealing with distress? Real workload, missing information, dependence on other people, caregiving and inaccessible tools may explain the problem. Do not infer laziness, ADHD, depression or a motivation deficit from an unfinished list.

Offer one relevant option in ordinary language. Respect a request to talk without exercises. A person asking for task help need not learn the book's terminology, adopt a new app or complete a productivity assessment.

## TASK-1: Capture without creating an obligation

**Use when:** commitments keep returning to mind or are scattered across messages and notes.

Try collecting the currently relevant items in an accessible place, then deciding what each needs. A captured item may be a possible idea, reference material, an appointment, a commitment or something to discard. One message can contain several kinds. Capture preserves an option; it does not make the option mandatory. Reading a message, accepting a request and completing it are different states. Processing incoming material does not require doing every request or keeping the inbox permanently empty. Set a realistic opportunity to look again, so the collection remains usable. A large backlog can be processed in small portions. This adapts 2017 sections 3.2–3.3 and 2021 sections 6.3.2 and 6.3.13 [S113, S114].

**Review:** is it easier to find the next commitment, and is maintaining the collection worth the effort? Avoid repeated exhaustive capture if it becomes another checking ritual.

## TASK-2: Choose a next action for an unclear project

**Use when:** an item is important but does not tell the person what to do next.

Identify an action within the person's control, the object of that action and an observable stopping point. If a dependency is missing, obtaining or clarifying it may be the action. If real thinking is required, specify the question and a useful output; do not pretend that all thinking can be eliminated. Keep the broad outcome in project notes, with only relevant next steps in the immediate view. This adapts 2017 sections 3.3–3.4.2 [S113].

**Wholly synthetic example:** “Organize the community workshop” might become “Draft two possible dates and ask the venue coordinator which is available.” This is a new fictional illustration, not a book example.

**Review:** did the action become startable? If it remains blocked, examine the dependency, capacity or reluctance instead of endlessly changing the verb. Clear wording does not guarantee action or remove legitimate distress. The later book itself treats wording as both a practice and a possible indicator of understanding, available resources and the wider work context, including fatigue or anticipated unpleasant consequences. This is the author's explanatory proposal, not an established causal test: better wording alone need not resolve those conditions. (section 6.2.6) [S114]

## TASK-3: Today's task list, realistic commitments and guilt

**Use when:** an aspirational list is being treated as a promise to finish everything.

Distinguish actual deadlines and consequences from desirable options. Choose a feasible next step after allowing for existing commitments, interruptions and rest. Keep other possibilities visible without giving every item an invented due date. When the day changes, renegotiate the plan. The size of the immediate view depends on the situation; a book's suggested task count is not a universal capacity limit. This adapts 2021 sections 6.2.10–6.2.11 [S114].

**Review:** compare what was feasible with what was attempted. Repeated overflow is information about the plan or workload. It is not a moral score or proof of insufficient effort. Extra planning cannot create unlimited capacity.

## TASK-4: Review, renegotiate and retire

**Use when:** the list is stale, repeatedly postponed or growing without useful selection.

At a manageable review opportunity, reconsider a few relevant items: are they still needed, is the next action clear, and is there a real opportunity to do it? Keep, clarify, schedule, renegotiate, defer with a reason or consciously drop an item. Distinguish waiting on someone else from being able to act now. A shorter review can be sufficient; do not make a missed review create a catch-up debt. On returning after a gap, improving a small part of the list can be enough; complete cleanup is not a prerequisite for using it again. This adapts 2017 sections 3.6 and 7.17, and 2021 sections 6.2.9 and 6.2.17 [S113, S114]. The later book's illustrative review durations are not adopted as proven optimal schedules.

**Review:** does the list support choices with less administrative burden? Renegotiate commitments that affect others. This is not permission to delete another person's records or disregard a consequential deadline.

## TASK-5: Identify a useful intermediate result

**Use when:** polishing or the size of an imagined finished product prevents progress.

Agree on an intermediate output that has value on its own, with an explicit stopping condition. Preserve required quality and safety; reduce optional scope or decoration. For example, a wholly synthetic workshop plan might first need an agreed date and capacity before a polished poster. This adapts 2017 section 4.1.4 [S113].

**Review:** did the intermediate output help the next decision? Do not turn the book's illustrative ratios, compressed deadlines or Pareto language into a prediction. Some work requires completeness, specialist checking or a fixed standard; “good enough” must respect those requirements.

## TASK-6: Connect an action to a chosen purpose

**Use when:** easy maintenance tasks consistently displace something the person cares about.

Ask what a proposed action would make possible, then select a modest step towards that purpose. Also recognize necessary maintenance and obligations: not every valuable action is enjoyable or directly advances an aspiration. The person may revise or reject a goal. This adapts 2021 section 6.4.4 [S114]; it is compatible with values discussion but is not itself validated ACT.

**Review:** did the action advance the chosen purpose? Counting completed tasks alone can reward trivial work. Rest, care and relationships need not justify themselves through a productivity metric.

## TASK-7: Protect a workable interval

**Use when:** optional interruptions repeatedly disrupt a chosen task.

Consider a reversible change to one distracting cue or a short, comfortable work interval with a stopping opportunity. Preserve channels needed for care, accessibility, on-call duties and urgent contact. The person chooses whether a timer helps. A fixed 25-minute cycle is an author-described technique, not an established optimal dose for everyone. This adapts 2017 section 3.7.3 and 2021 section 6.1.6 [S113, S114].

**Review:** assess useful progress, fatigue and communication costs. Do not apply an attention exercise during driving or another activity requiring continuous monitoring. Do not use the books to prescribe sleep treatment or interpret a wearable's sleep stages.

## TASK-8: Self-observation and a small personal experiment

**Use when:** the person wants to test a small change rather than install a complete system.

Choose a low-risk change and a relevant observation: for example, whether an agreed next step was easier to start. Decide when to reconsider it and what would make it too burdensome. If wanted, use one brief note or a simple optional check rather than several parallel trackers. Record “not tried” or missing observations as such; neither means failure or zero benefit. Keep, modify or stop based on usefulness and cost. This adapts 2021 sections 5.3 and 5.5–5.6 [S114].

The project's suggestion to change **one thing at first** is an original simplification, not a quotation or exact reproduction of Dorofeev's multi-component approach: section 5.8 describes trying a small group of three to five components [S114]. Neither number is treated here as an empirically optimal prescription. A few days of observation can guide a preference; they do not establish causation, durable habit formation or a guaranteed time to benefit. Workload changes, expectations, practice, selection and ordinary variation remain alternatives. Agree on review timing appropriate to the change, and stop sooner if it worsens functioning or distress. Do not pressure a person to test an unwanted or unsafe action to prove their objection.

## What independent evidence can support: reminders, planning and thought fuel

These checks concern narrow constructs, not the books' complete system or this assistant. Targeted search and access check: 2026-10-29; no systematic search, complete correction/retraction audit or comprehensive recent-evidence review.

- **External reminders:** Gilbert's four online experiments report improved delayed-intention performance with reminders [S115]. That supports a limited memory aid rationale, not a claim that any task app treats distress or improves all daily functioning. Only the primary abstract and bibliographic/licence record were appraised here.
- **Specific plans:** the indexed primary abstract of Masicampo and Baumeister reports reduced goal-related cognitive interference with specific plans, while emotion changes were not necessary [S116]. “Write a plan and anxiety will disappear” exceeds that reading. Full methods and replication evidence were not appraised.
- **Resource metaphors:** Vohs and colleagues' preregistered study across 36 laboratories did not establish the hypothesized depletion effect in confirmatory analysis; exploratory results differed [S117]. Treat “thought fuel” as an organizing metaphor, not a measured tank or an individual explanation of fatigue. This study does not show that fatigue is unreal.

## Boundaries that change the response

Book anecdotes, participant testimonials, metaphors and sales language are not controlled efficacy evidence. The 2017 discussion of wording invokes NLP; that reference does not validate NLP or establish a special power of particular words. The chapter 6 maturity ladder in the later book is an author framework, not a diagnostic scale or a hierarchy of personal worth. The later book's suggestions about deliberately missing tasks are not adopted as an exposure protocol. Help negotiate workload and preserve consequential duties instead. No clinical causal claim is inferred from that discussion. [S113, S114]

Task support may accompany distress, but it does not diagnose or treat depression, anxiety, ADHD, insomnia or burnout. If persistent difficulties materially affect everyday functioning, use the [assessment and referral guide](33_ASSESSMENT_AND_REFERRAL.md). If the request is simply practical, keep the response practical. Existing private-record authorization is required before saving an observation; the exercise itself does not authorize storage, reminders or background monitoring.

For future maintenance, revisit an outcome claim when a relevant new study/correction appears, and recheck the exact edition before attributing a changed instruction to the author. Leave unstudied clinical and measurement claims outside this module.
