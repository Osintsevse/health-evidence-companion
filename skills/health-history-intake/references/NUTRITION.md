# Nutrition reasoning, food diaries and safe dietary change

Checked 2026-10-09. This is original educational guidance for public use, not an individual diet prescription. Nutrition supports clinical care; it does not replace indicated medicines or assessment. Use the person's goals and preferences, preserve uncertainty, and involve a qualified dietitian for therapeutic diets. Medical information cannot establish a diagnosis or nutritional fitness from a diary, photograph, weight, or laboratory number alone.

## Begin with the decision and the minimum questions

Ask what the person wants: estimate a meal, understand a label, review their eating pattern, manage a diagnosed condition, or plan a feasible substitution. Ask permission before discussing weight or calorie tracking. A person seeking an explanation of a label does not necessarily want weight loss. Avoid moral words such as clean, cheating, guilty, or bad foods. Familiar foods can often fit a useful pattern; affordability and enjoyment affect whether change lasts.

Use a short sequence, adding detail only when it changes the answer:

1. Establish age group, pregnancy or lactation, major diagnosed conditions, relevant medicines, known allergy, and whether eating or drinking currently causes concerning symptoms. Ask about prescribed restrictions and the treating team's plan before proposing a therapeutic diet.
2. Clarify the goal, available time, budget, cooking facilities, culture, dietary preferences, and whether tracking feels helpful or distressing. Identify unintentional weight loss, inability to eat, swallowing difficulty, or compensatory behaviors before discussing deficits.
3. For calculations, obtain the actual food or product, country and label, amount consumed, and whether weights describe raw, cooked, drained, or edible food. Ask about oils, sauces, drinks, alcohol, and recipe yield without assuming they were consumed.
4. For diary review, ask the dates covered and whether these were typical days. Establish which meals are complete, which amounts are estimated, and which entries are missing. Hunger, symptoms and circumstances can be included voluntarily; identifiers are unnecessary.
5. Agree on one or two actionable questions, describe what is known, and offer a provisional analysis with explicit assumptions. Revisit it when new information arrives.

For acute breathing difficulty or collapse after food, stop the diet interview and direct emergency help. For rapid restriction-related deterioration, prioritize medical assessment rather than a more accurate spreadsheet. Referral thresholds and particular safeguards appear below. S333 S330

## Estimating energy and macronutrients honestly

Food calculations use several different objects: the amount offered, the amount eaten, the edible portion, nutrient composition, and energy conversion factors. Separate them. A photo may suggest food types but cannot reliably measure hidden oil, serving mass, fillings or recipe composition. Offer a range if the user wants an estimate and agrees to assumptions; do not turn a guess into an exact factual entry.

Prefer the matching package label for a specific product, then an appropriate national composition database for generic food, then a clearly marked approximation. Brand formulations and regional products can differ. A database entry for cooked rice must not be applied directly to an equal mass of dry rice. Water absorbed during cooking changes nutrient density per gram. Water lost during roasting concentrates the remaining food; it does not create energy. Removed skin, bones, discarded cooking water, drained packing liquid and leftover frying oil change the amount actually eaten.

A practical arithmetic method is nutrient amount = label nutrient per 100 g multiplied by consumed edible grams divided by 100. If a label is per serving, multiply by the number of those servings eaten. Keep denominators in every table. A 150 g portion of a fictional product listing 180 kcal per 100 g contributes 270 label kcal. This is arithmetic, not a claim that the product's actual energy is measured without error.

For a mixed recipe, record ingredients with their matching raw or cooked state; sum their nutrients, then allocate by the consumed fraction of the finished edible batch. If the finished batch weighs 900 g and 225 g is eaten, allocate one quarter, provided the mixture is reasonably uniform. Do not divide by the raw ingredient weight after evaporation. Where a dish is uneven, such as a stew with different meat portions, the fraction of total mass may be a poor nutrient proxy. Describe that limitation instead of supplying false precision.

The familiar approximation is energy in kcal = 4 × protein grams + 4 × available carbohydrate grams + 9 × fat grams. Alcohol contributes approximately 7 kcal/g. These are general metabolizable-energy factors, not an exact physiological measurement for every ingredient or person. Fibre, polyols, organic acids and some modified fats have different factors; analytical methods and food-specific factors matter. Do not assign 4 kcal to every gram labelled total carbohydrate, then add fibre energy again. S323

EU regulation separately provides factors including fibre 2 kcal/g, most polyols 2.4 kcal/g and erythritol 0 kcal/g. The label's declared energy is often more appropriate than reconstructing it from rounded macros. Legal definitions and rounding can explain small discrepancies. If a product has a large unexplained discrepancy, recheck units, preparation instructions and label transcription rather than altering the published value. S324

Alcohol energy includes ethanol plus carbohydrate or other ingredients in the beverage. A calorie estimate is not a recommendation to drink; alcohol can complicate triglyceride management and disease care. A glass is not a standardized volume. If calculating, ask volume and alcohol by volume, and state the conversion assumptions. Record alcohol separately from carbohydrate rather than treating beer or wine as carbohydrate alone. S327

## Labels, carbohydrates and multilingual preservation

US total carbohydrate includes fibre; sugars are components rather than extra grams to add on top. Added sugars are included within total sugars. The FDA serving size describes a customary amount, not an instruction to eat that amount. Percent Daily Value provides reference context, not a personalized prescription based on the person's energy needs. S325

EU carbohydrate denotes carbohydrates metabolized by humans, including polyols, while fibre is declared separately. Subtracting fibre again from this number can underestimate carbohydrate. Net carbs is not a universally interchangeable analytical quantity. Preserve the original label heading and jurisdiction; do not apply a US subtraction rule to a Serbian, Russian or EU label without verifying its definition. This packet explains EU and US conventions; it does not assert that Serbian or Russian regulations are identical. S324

Keep original text beside a translation when ambiguity matters. The [term glossary](terminology.json) includes English, Serbian Latin and Russian nutrient headings. These translations help reading but do not establish regulatory equivalence. Retain g, mg, µg, kcal and kJ, decimal commas, denominator, package size, and whether the declaration is for the product as sold or prepared. Convert a decimal comma deliberately, not by deleting punctuation. One kcal is 4.184 kJ; do not confuse a label with 840 kJ for one with 840 kcal.

Salt and sodium are different quantities. EU salt equivalent is sodium multiplied by 2.5. Thus fictional salt 1.0 g corresponds to sodium 0.4 g, or 400 mg. Preserve both original and converted values. Do not translate 1 g salt into 1 g sodium. Added sugar, free sugar and total sugar also answer different questions: honey and juice sugars count as free sugars in WHO guidance, while whole fruit and plain milk cannot be evaluated simply by calling all their sugars added. S324 S322

| Reported information | Appropriate interpretation | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Per 100 g, portion unknown | Compare products on equal basis; ask consumed mass | Calling it the person's total intake |
| Per 30 g serving, 60 g consumed | Multiply by two | Treating whole package as one serving |
| Raw pasta weight but cooked composition | Request matching state or recipe yield | Mixing denominators |
| Carbohydrate with fibre separately | Verify national convention | Automatic fibre subtraction |
| Milk label total sugar | Explain natural versus added distinction | Labelling all sugar as added |
| Blurred number or missing unit | Mark unreadable and request optional clearer evidence | Guessing a precise value |

## Reviewing a diary without inventing it

A diary is a sample of reported behavior, not proof of usual intake or micronutrient adequacy. Short periods, unusual travel days, missing weekends, uncertain portions and selective recording can change conclusions. Do not attribute missing entries to dishonesty. Do not infer an unrecorded breakfast was skipped, or add a standard lunch because most people eat one. Use “not recorded” until confirmed.

Maintain three layers: reported facts, calculation assumptions, and interpretations. Preserve a correction by explaining what changed. If a user clarifies that a rice weight was dry rather than cooked, recompute the affected entry and totals, and state that earlier figures used the wrong state. If they reveal pregnancy, CKD or insulin use, reconsider recommendations as well as arithmetic. More precise calorie estimates do not automatically make earlier advice safe.

Identify likely issues as questions, not verdicts: few recorded vegetables or legumes; high use of salty sauces; little variety; long meal gaps; substantial sweetened drinks; protein sources missing from some meals; hunger despite apparently sufficient energy; or expensive foods that make the plan impractical. A low recorded total may reflect incomplete records, illness, restriction or an unusually quiet day. Explain these competing possibilities. A diet diary cannot diagnose iron deficiency, diabetes or an eating disorder.

Offer a report that the person can check:

- Purpose and coverage: the question, dates, typical versus atypical days, and completeness.
- Known inputs: actual products, stated portions, recipe states, and relevant prescribed constraints.
- Estimates: energy, protein, carbohydrate definition, fat and optional fibre, sodium or salt, with units and uncertainty. Exclude incomplete days from an unqualified daily average.
- Patterns: two or three observations supported by entries; strengths and opportunities stated neutrally.
- Options: a small number of affordable substitutions, expected purpose, and important exceptions.
- Safety and follow-up: unresolved symptoms, who should review them, and what information would materially change the analysis.

Do not hide uncertainty inside a single confidence percentage. Use a range whose assumptions are explained, or decline a numerical total if the missing ingredients dominate. When the goal is a healthier pattern, improving label comprehension or regular meal access may be more useful than weighing every item. If tracking increases anxiety, consider a nonnumerical meal-pattern discussion and appropriate support.

## Food patterns and useful substitutions

WHO's adult population guidance includes at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables daily, at least 25 g naturally occurring fibre for people older than 10, free sugars below 10% of energy, saturated fat no more than 10% of energy, trans fat no more than 1%, and salt below 5 g daily. These are population reference points; they are not a universal menu or an individualized therapeutic prescription. Children's needs vary with age. Use a gradual, tolerated increase in fibre rather than treating a missed target as failure. S322

AHA's 2026 pattern emphasizes varied vegetables and fruit, whole grains, suitable protein sources, replacing saturated with unsaturated fats, less sodium and added sugar, and limited alcohol. The food replacing a nutrient matters: replacing butter with an unsaturated oil differs from replacing it with refined sweets. Energy adequacy still matters in frailty or undernutrition. S339

| Goal discussed with the clinical team | Practical option to consider | Decision-changing exception |
|---|---|---|
| Elevated LDL | Replace some butter or fatty meat with non-tropical plant oil, beans, lentils, fish or suitable lower-fat dairy | Allergy, very low intake, renal restrictions, or severe triglyceride plan may alter choice |
| More fibre | Add oats, legumes, whole grains, vegetables or whole fruit that the person tolerates | Swallowing difficulty or a prescribed gastrointestinal diet needs specific advice |
| Less sodium | Compare breads, canned foods and sauces; choose lower-salt alternatives; use herbs or lemon | Potassium-based salt substitutes need review in CKD or heart failure |
| Less free sugar | Replace some sweetened drinks with water; choose unsweetened alternatives | Hypoglycemia rescue and prescribed nutritional supplements have different roles |
| Elevated triglycerides | Review alcohol, sweetened drinks, portions and secondary causes with clinician | Severe elevation needs a different therapeutic approach |
| Limited budget | Use frozen vegetables, suitable canned pulses, seasonal foods and batch cooking | Check salt, allergy and food-storage requirements |

These are examples for shared decision-making, not a list of banned foods. Ask which change is easiest this week. A whole pattern matters more than declaring one ingredient protective or toxic. Do not claim that nutrition will normalize every LDL result or permit stopping a statin. A person with high cardiovascular risk may need both lifestyle support and medicines.

DASH is a flexible eating pattern using common foods. Its serving tables vary by energy requirement; a published example is not a calorie prescription for everyone. Discuss lower-sodium choices alongside fruit, vegetables, whole grains and suitable protein foods. A blood pressure reading does not authorize altering medication, urgent fluid changes or a potassium target. Kidney disease and medicines can make general potassium-rich advice inappropriate. ## Conditions and life stages that change advice

**Triglycerides.** Confirm the laboratory unit, fasting context, repeat status, clinician interpretation, alcohol use and relevant conditions before recommending a pattern. ACC separates moderate from severe hypertriglyceridemia; the latter can require a supervised very-low-fat approach, rather than routinely adding oils and nuts. Values at or above 500 mg/dL (about 5.6 mmol/L) warrant timely clinical review, particularly at or above 1000 mg/dL (about 11.3 mmol/L). Severe abdominal pain or persistent vomiting with marked elevation needs urgent assessment for possible pancreatitis. Do not generate a therapeutic fat-gram prescription or replace prescribed lipid treatment with supplements. S327

**Diabetes.** There is no single ideal macronutrient distribution for everyone; goals and medicines matter. Different eating patterns can be discussed with a dietitian. A carbohydrate estimate should preserve label definitions and the person's taught counting method. Chat cannot safely derive insulin doses from it. Large meal changes, fasting or exercise may need a medication review, especially with insulin or sulfonylureas; follow a verified existing hypoglycemia plan rather than improvising treatment. Very-low-carbohydrate eating or prolonged fasting with an SGLT2 inhibitor needs clinician review because ketoacidosis risk can change. Nutrition therapy is complementary to indicated diabetes treatment. The 2026 ADA nutrition source was available through indexed excerpts, not full direct retrieval; keep specific treatment claims within that limit. S338 S340

**CKD.** There is no single renal diet. Ask stage or current kidney information, dialysis status, laboratory trends and the treating team's instructions. Protein needs differ before and during dialysis, and insufficient intake can cause harm. Potassium, phosphorus, sodium and fluids need individualized assessment; do not ban all beans, fruit or vegetables, or prescribe a generic low-protein diet. Salt substitutes, potassium-containing supplements and high-protein products warrant review. Specialist dietitian input can reconcile renal constraints with diabetes, cardiovascular goals and adequate energy. S328

**Heart failure.** Do not impose automatic sodium or fluid restriction. NICE recommends assessing intake and particular indications, with ongoing review; it advises avoiding potassium-containing salt substitutes. Ask about the existing plan and deterioration. Breathlessness at rest or rapidly worsening swelling requires medical assessment rather than a meal-plan adjustment. A diary cannot determine congestion, electrolyte status or the correct diuretic dose. S329

**Older adults and undernutrition.** Unintentional weight loss, low intake, swallowing difficulty or loss of function changes the priority from restriction to assessment and adequate nutrition. Screen contextually; normal or high BMI does not exclude nutritional risk. Little or no intake for more than five days is a reason to obtain clinical advice about nutrition support and refeeding risk. Do not provide a rapid home refeeding schedule, electrolyte dosing or generic protein target. A dietitian can adapt texture, meal access and support, while swallowing problems need appropriate assessment. S337

**Childhood.** Growth and development require age-specific advice. Avoid adult calorie deficits, fixed adult macros and interpretation from a single weight measurement. Ask age, growth concerns, feeding skills, selectivity and clinician input. Parents need support without blame. Broad exclusion diets, failure to grow, dehydration or persistent feeding difficulty warrant pediatric review. Ordinary whole-food ideas can be discussed, but infant feeding and therapeutic child diets require their own guidance. S331 S332

**Pregnancy and lactation.** Pregnancy is not an indication for chat-generated weight loss. NICE advises against intentional weight loss during pregnancy. Discuss variety, food safety and the local antenatal supplement plan with the maternity team; vomiting, inability to keep fluids down or poor intake needs assessment. Lactation does not require a special restrictive diet, although adequate nutrition and individual needs matter. Do not promise a food or supplement will increase milk supply or infer infant adequacy from maternal calorie totals. S331

**Vegetarian or vegan eating.** Ask which foods are excluded and which fortified products or supplements are actually used. Check reliable B12 sources and review calcium, vitamin D, iodine, iron and protein variety. The fact that a product is plant-based does not establish nutritional equivalence to dairy or adequate fortification. Read the exact carton. Pregnancy, children and frail older adults benefit from qualified guidance when planning exclusions or correcting low intake. Do not prescribe high-dose supplements from a diary alone. S332

## Allergy, intolerance and restrictive eating

Food allergy and intolerance are distinct; symptoms and timing overlap, so a chat description cannot prove either. Allergy can involve hives, swelling, breathing difficulty or circulatory symptoms; intolerance commonly involves digestive symptoms and is not an immune allergy. A symptom diary can support assessment, but symptom-free ingestion once does not clear a suspected allergen for home challenge. Follow a specialist allergy plan and the exact prescribed emergency product. Suspected anaphylaxis requires emergency help immediately; do not ask for a photo or conduct calorie analysis first. S333 S334

For nonurgent suspected intolerance, ask the food, amount, timing, repeatability and other causes; encourage clinical review before long-term exclusion. Commercial intolerance tests can recommend unnecessary multiple exclusions and have limited accuracy evidence. In children, exclusions can compromise growth and nutrition. Do not label symptoms psychosomatic because testing is incomplete, and do not diagnose an allergy from a positive panel without the clinical history. S334

Restriction may be a nutritional choice, medical requirement, sensory difficulty, food insecurity or part of an eating disorder. Ask sensitively about fear of eating, distress around weight, compulsive tracking, binge episodes, purging, laxatives, exercise used as compensation and rapid change in intake. Eating disorders can occur across body sizes; do not use BMI or a screening questionnaire alone to dismiss concern. When restriction is suspected, avoid helping optimize a deficit, supplying fasting targets or complimenting rapid weight loss. Offer assessment and practical support without diagnosing. Fainting, severe weakness, inability to drink, severe dehydration, suspected electrolyte disturbance or marked deterioration requires urgent medical care. S330

## Supplements, microbiome claims and processing classifications

A food diary can identify a question about adequacy, not prove that a supplement is required. Ask the exact ingredient, dose, product, indication and relevant medicines or illness. Discuss targeted replacement with a clinician when deficiency or life-stage guidance supports it; do not treat supplements as insurance against an otherwise inadequate diet. Product purity, marketing language, regulatory availability and clinical effectiveness are different questions. Avoid recommending a supplement as a substitute for established lipid, diabetes or heart-failure treatment.

Probiotic effects depend on strain, condition and outcome. A result for one product does not establish a class-wide benefit or justify a personalized microbiome treatment. ODS describes serious infection concerns in severely ill or immunocompromised people and special concerns in preterm infants. Do not offer a generic probiotic regimen in these groups. A diversity score or proprietary stool report is not enough to select a treatment; claims need a demonstrated clinical outcome and population match. S335

Processing categories can inform a discussion but cannot grade every food's nutritional value from the number of ingredients. Hall's 2019 randomized crossover inpatient study included 20 adults and two weeks on each diet. Intake was about 508 kcal/day higher on the tested ultra-processed diet. This supports a short-term causal effect of those menus on intake, not proof that all processed foods are harmful or that every additive independently causes disease. Differences in non-beverage energy density, eating rate and food properties complicate mechanism attribution. The study does not establish long-term cardiovascular outcomes or pediatric effects. S336

Avoid presenting a short trial as a completed review of the entire field. Some convenient foods support affordability, safety and adequacy, including fortified products that may fall within processing classifications. Use observable features such as fibre, sodium, saturated fat, protein and accessibility alongside processing, rather than instructing people to discard everything packaged. Neither a probiotic nor an unprocessed label establishes therapeutic benefit.

## Follow-up and referral

A qualified dietitian should be registered or appropriately credentialed in the relevant jurisdiction, with expertise matching the problem. Refer for CKD, severe triglycerides, complex diabetes treatment, food allergy with multiple exclusions, pregnancy with restricted intake, childhood growth concerns, unexplained weight loss, frailty or suspected disordered eating. Medical symptoms also need the appropriate clinician; dietetic support alone cannot rule out serious illness. S328 S327 S337

Agree on a review outcome that fits the goal: easier meals, adequate intake, better symptom understanding, a verified label comparison, or follow-up laboratory assessment ordered by the clinician. Do not promise a particular weight or biomarker change. Ask what worked and what barriers appeared. Update the plan when symptoms, medicines, pregnancy status, diagnosis, budget or new diary evidence changes. Preserve useful progress even when numerical targets are not met.
