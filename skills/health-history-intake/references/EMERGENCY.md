# Lay first aid and emergency routing

Reviewed 2026-10-09. This module helps a nonclinician recognise danger, summon help and take limited first-aid action. Recognition is not diagnosis. It does not cover professional resuscitation, birth resuscitation, personal prescribing or every emergency. Apply the emergency service's instructions for the person's location, current local training and the actual rescue product. Guidelines share important principles but differ in some sequences and age definitions.

## The first reply: act before explaining

When a situation may be immediately life-threatening, start with three to five short actions. The first sentence should say what to do now. Do not first ask for a complete history, photograph, blood-pressure reading, diagnostic test or search permission. Explain the reason in one sentence if needed. Add the short informational disclaimer after the actions; it must not become an obstacle to help. A useful operational target is roughly 60–120 words for the initial emergency response, adjusted for an essential age-specific manoeuvre. This is an editorial target, not a researched therapeutic threshold.

1. Check that approaching is safe; avoid traffic, electricity, fire, fumes and violence.
2. Call the local emergency service now, ideally on speakerphone. Give the exact location first, then what happened, approximate age, responsiveness and breathing. Send a specific bystander to call or bring an AED when available.
3. Take the immediately relevant action: CPR for an unresponsive person breathing abnormally, pressure for severe external bleeding, age-appropriate choking care or the prescribed adrenaline auto-injector for suspected anaphylaxis.
4. Stay with the person and follow the dispatcher. Report deterioration immediately. If safe and another person can do it, arrange access for responders.

Do not delay calling to establish which diagnosis is correct. Do not give anything by mouth to someone who cannot swallow safely. Do not transport a deteriorating person by having them drive. A photo comes after safety and care, if it has any useful role at all. These safeguards implement early-call and first-aid priorities. S267 S268 S277

## Location, phone numbers and dispatcher authority

Use the person's current country, not profile language, nationality or an old address. If the country is unknown, say “Call your local emergency number now” and ask location alongside the action. A helper can verify the number while another starts care. Do not keep an emergency user waiting through a web search. Verification belongs in preparation or parallel assistance; do not call emergency services merely to test a number.

Verified examples are 112 throughout the EU; 999 in the UK guidance; and 194 for an ambulance in Serbia, supported by Serbia's government and current US State Department country information. The Serbian government also lists 112 for emergency intervention, but this module does not assume its dispatch implementation or access is identical to the EU system. US Poison Control's 1-800-222-1222 and 911 instructions are US routes. NHS 111 is a UK advice service, not an international poison-centre number. Availability and accessible alternatives need local verification. S289 S290 S291 S278

Regional technique variants should be learned in advance. During a live emergency, give one coherent algorithm appropriate to local training/dispatcher instructions, not a menu of competing techniques or a debate that delays action.

A live dispatcher may adapt instructions to age, trauma, rescuer ability, location, products and equipment. Follow those instructions instead of combining chat algorithms. Tell the dispatcher if the instruction cannot be performed or seems inconsistent with circumstances or a prescribed rescue plan. The assistant should support that conversation, not compete with it. If disconnected, reconnect and continue necessary care. Do not promise that a silent call automatically identifies location everywhere. S267 S269

## Suspected stroke or transient ischaemic attack

First actions: **Call an ambulance now for sudden stroke-like symptoms, including symptoms that just stopped.** Note symptom onset, or when the person was last known well. Keep them safe and at rest, monitor breathing and responsiveness, and follow the dispatcher. Do not let them drive. Tell responders about relevant medicines, particularly blood thinners, without delaying the call. S275

Recognise sudden facial droop, one-sided weakness or numbness, abnormal speech or inability to find words. FAST is a recognition aid, not a rule-out test: sudden vision loss, severe unusual headache or marked new balance difficulty also needs urgent assessment. Brief improvement does not establish a harmless cause; a possible TIA needs assessment despite resolved deficits. NHS specifically routes signs within the previous 24 hours to an ambulance even when they stop. Older resolved events still need prompt clinical assessment; do not invent a safe waiting period. S275

Do not recommend aspirin as automatic stroke treatment, a home swallow test, food/drink when swallowing is uncertain, or rapid blood-pressure manipulation. Do not ask for balance challenges or symptom videos before calling. A normal home reading or young age does not clear an acute neurological problem. Migraine, panic or a trapped nerve may enter later reasoning but cannot remotely clear a new sudden deficit. Distinguishing haemorrhage, ischaemia and mimics belongs to clinical assessment. The immediate task is recognition and routing, without numerical diagnostic confidence.

## Suspected heart attack and concerning chest symptoms

First actions: **Call emergency services for new squeezing/pressure-like chest discomfort, spreading pain or severe breathing difficulty.** Stop activity and rest in a comfortable supported position. Do not drive yourself. Stay with the person and monitor responsiveness and breathing. If they become unresponsive with abnormal breathing, use the arrest pathway. S276

Heart attack can include sweating, nausea, breathlessness or an indigestion-like feeling. Dramatic pain is not required. Chat cannot distinguish heart attack from other dangerous chest conditions. Improvement, a normal wearable reading or a prior anxiety diagnosis does not settle it. Describe uncertainty without forcing every brief isolated sensation into an ambulance route: establish onset and associated warnings, then arrange appropriate assessment for less severe concerns. New, persistent, recurrent or changing symptoms deserve review.

Aspirin is a guideline option for suitable adults with suspected cardiac chest pain, but **not a universal chat order**. The NHS recommendation includes an allergy restriction; bleeding, other possible emergencies, age and local protocol can change applicability. This international packet routes aspirin to dispatcher advice; do not delay calling to find it, and do not use it for undifferentiated neurological symptoms or an unsafe swallow. Help with the person's own prescribed angina rescue medicine under their plan; do not lend a nitrate, create a dosing schedule or give extra antihypertensives to lower a bare reading. S276

## Unresponsive person: breathing, CPR and AED

First actions: **If the person is unresponsive, call emergency services now on speakerphone.** Open the airway and check breathing briefly, no longer than 10 seconds. If breathing is absent, abnormal or only gasping, start CPR and follow the dispatcher. Send someone for an AED. If breathing is clearly normal, maintain the airway, use a recovery position when appropriate and monitor continuously. Suspected major trauma needs guidance on movement, but airway and breathing take priority. S267 S269

Adult compressions: place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest, the other on top, and push vertically at 100–120 per minute, 5–6 cm deep. Allow full recoil and minimise pauses. If trained and able, use 30 compressions then two breaths, each just enough for chest rise. If untrained or unable to give breaths, perform continuous compressions with telephone guidance. Lay rescuers should not delay CPR searching for a pulse. Occasional gasps, snoring-like sounds or unusual panting after collapse are not reassuring normal breathing. S292

Switch on the AED, expose the chest, apply pads as pictured and follow prompts. Keep everyone clear during analysis and shock, then resume CPR as instructed. Another rescuer can apply pads during compressions. The AED decides whether to advise a shock; the helper does not diagnose the rhythm. Continue until professionals take over, clear normal signs of life return, the scene becomes unsafe or you cannot continue. Do not abandon the person for an extensive AED search when calling and CPR are immediate priorities. S292

For infants and children, breaths are particularly important because arrest commonly follows breathing problems. Use paediatric pads/attenuation if available, following AED age/product instructions; do not withhold an available AED merely because paediatric equipment is absent. Avoid pads touching. ERC/RCUK 2025 begins with five rescue breaths, then compressions and breaths: 15:2 for specifically paediatric-trained rescuers, otherwise 30:2. AHA 2025 starts with compressions; its public lone-lay-rescuer teaching uses 30 compressions with breaths. Do not blend the starts: follow local training and the dispatcher. S269 S270 S271

Compress a child's chest about one-third its depth, approximately 5 cm, and an infant's approximately 4 cm, at 100–120 per minute. Use one or two hands for a child as appropriate. AHA 2025 recommends the heel of one hand or two thumbs encircling the chest for infant CPR; two-finger compressions are no longer recommended there. Infants are under one year in this teaching; AHA's child algorithm runs to puberty, whereas ERC/RCUK paediatric scope extends to 18 with adult-looking adolescents managed practically as adults. Birth resuscitation is separate. Do not waste urgent time debating a borderline category. S270 S269

## Choking: cough strength and age change the action

First check whether the person can breathe and cough effectively. **If they can cough strongly or speak/cry, encourage coughing and watch closely.** Do not immediately deliver thrusts or force water into the mouth. **If they cannot speak/cry, have a weak/silent cough or cannot breathe, summon help and begin choking care.** Ask a bystander to call while you act. Stop manoeuvres when obstruction clears; reassess breathing. S273

For a responsive adult or child over one year, lean them forward, support the chest and give up to five blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of the hand. If ineffective, give up to five abdominal thrusts: stand or kneel behind, place a fist above the navel and below the breastbone, grasp it and pull inward and upward. Alternate sets until cleared or unresponsive. For known pregnancy or an abdomen you cannot encircle, Red Cross guidance uses chest thrusts instead. Use dispatcher technique guidance if uncertain. S273

For a responsive infant under one year, support head and neck, hold face down along the forearm/thigh with the head below the chest and give up to five back blows. Turn face up with support and give up to five chest thrusts; alternate. **Never use infant abdominal thrusts or hang/shake the baby upside down.** ERC/RCUK 2025 specifies two thumbs encircling the chest for infant thrusts; AHA 2025 specifies the heel of one hand. Older public Red Cross materials still describe two fingers. Use current local teaching and live dispatcher instructions. The shared priority is back blows/chest thrusts, rather than an adult abdominal manoeuvre. S269 S270 S272 S274

If unresponsive, lower safely to a firm surface and start the local CPR pathway. Before breaths, remove only an object clearly visible and accessible; never sweep blindly. Do not delay established care to buy, locate or assemble a marketed suction device: ERC/RCUK finds insufficient evidence to recommend for or against these. After successful abdominal thrusts or CPR chest compressions, arrange assessment for possible injury. Persistent cough, swallowing trouble or breathing problems needs assessment even if some food came out. S269 S268

## Severe external bleeding

First actions: **Call emergency services and press firmly on the bleeding wound now.** Use a dressing or clean cloth; protect yourself from blood if practical without delaying pressure. Maintain pressure. For life-threatening arm/leg bleeding not controlled by pressure, use an available purpose-made tourniquet under training, product instructions or dispatcher guidance. Keep the person warm and monitor breathing and responsiveness. S268

Pooling/rapidly flowing blood, amputation or collapse makes this urgent; no invented millilitre estimate is needed. A tourniquet is for a limb, not neck or torso. Guidance places it above the wound, clear of joints, tight enough to stop bleeding; note time and do not loosen it. Trained packing/hemostatic dressings can help appropriate wounds, but do not turn this into probing a chest, abdominal or head wound. Do not remove embedded objects or push exposed bone back in: press around and describe to the dispatcher. Do not interrupt bleeding control to clean or photograph. S268 S284 S294

## Suspected anaphylaxis and the prescribed auto-injector

First actions: **Use the person's prescribed adrenaline/epinephrine auto-injector promptly for suspected anaphylaxis, following that device's instructions; call an ambulance.** Tell the dispatcher “suspected anaphylaxis.” Lie flat; with breathing difficulty, sit with legs extended instead of standing/walking. Stay with them. If severe symptoms persist after five minutes and a second prescribed injector is available, ERC/RCUK supports a second injection; follow the dispatcher and rescue plan. S268 S287

Danger signs include throat/tongue swelling, altered voice, breathing difficulty, wheeze, faintness or collapse after possible allergen exposure. Do not require hives before acting. Rash alone without airway, breathing or circulation symptoms requires a different assessment, with monitoring for progression and the existing allergy plan. Antihistamines and asthma inhalers do not replace adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Improvement after injection still requires emergency assessment. Pregnant people should lie on their left side; an unconscious person breathing normally needs an airway-protecting position. S177 S268

The injection site is the outer thigh. Cap colours, activation and hold time are not interchangeable. The inspected UK EpiPen 0.3 mg SmPC revised July 2026 specifies a three-second hold; this is not an instruction for every injector or selection of strength for a child. Use the prescribed device's actual label, not an ampoule or improvised injection. Do not calculate paediatric adrenaline doses from chat. S288

## Seizure

First actions: **Protect from injury and time the seizure.** Clear hazards, cushion the head and stay. Do not restrain or place anything in the mouth. After convulsions stop, check breathing: recovery position if breathing normally, CPR if unresponsive with abnormal breathing. Call for a first seizure, prolonged seizure, repeated seizures without recovery, serious injury or breathing difficulty. S282

Use five minutes as the recognised prolonged-seizure emergency threshold, or sooner under an established plan or when longer than usual. If start time is unknown or danger signs exist, do not wait another five minutes. A known epilepsy plan may allow a trained carer to give prescribed rescue medicine; do not improvise sedatives, routes or doses. A typical brief known seizure with full recovery may follow its care plan rather than automatically require an ambulance. No food, drink or tablets until fully recovered and swallowing safely. Sudden collapse with brief jerking can accompany cardiac arrest: assess breathing rather than assuming epilepsy. S282 S267

## Poisoning and overdose, even without symptoms

First actions: **For collapse, seizure, breathing difficulty or inability to awaken, call emergency services now and use the CPR/airway pathway.** For possible poisoning without these signs, contact the local poison or urgent medical service immediately. Give substance, formulation, amount if known, time, approximate age and symptoms. Keep packaging available without delay. Do not induce vomiting or give a home antidote. S277 S278

Do not use chat toxicity calculations to reassure about uncertain amounts, child exposures, mixed medicines or intentional overdose. Paracetamol/acetaminophen overdose can initially feel completely normal. Suspected excess, repeated excess or overlapping cold remedies needs immediate expert assessment, not waiting for pain or jaundice. Hospital timed blood tests and antidote protocols do not mean staying home until a test time. Intentional ingestion needs prompt medical and safety support; remain with the person when safe and avoid judgement. S279

For possible opioid overdose, call and support breathing/CPR; give available naloxone under the actual product and local instructions. Do not delay ventilation/compressions while searching for it. Naloxone is temporary; awakening does not cancel the ambulance because respiratory depression can recur. It does not reverse every intoxicant. Do not use coffee, cold showers, forced walking, charcoal or alcohol. S280

Move from fumes to fresh air only if safe; do not enter a contaminated enclosed space without specialist rescue equipment. Chemical eye/skin exposure needs prompt irrigation and poison guidance. US Poison Control advises at least 15–20 minutes for eyes and 15 minutes for skin. Oral advice differs: NHS advises no food/drink; US Poison Control allows a small amount of water/milk in certain caustic exposures only when alert and able to swallow. Do not universalise dilution: obtain substance-specific advice. Never give anything orally with impaired consciousness, convulsions or unsafe swallowing. S278 S277

## Hypoglycaemia: swallowing is decisive

First actions for someone awake and swallowing safely: **Give fast-acting glucose under the established diabetes plan; check glucose if readily available without delaying treatment.** Adult first-aid guidance uses 15–20 g glucose. Recheck in 10–15 minutes and repeat if still low under the plan; use the plan's longer-acting food after recovery. Get help if not improving. S281 S293

For drowsiness, unresponsiveness, convulsions or unsafe swallowing: **Call emergency services, give nothing by mouth, protect the airway and check breathing.** A trained helper may give prescribed glucagon using that device/rescue plan. Use CPR for abnormal breathing. Do not smear gel, honey or sugar into an unconscious mouth; do not give insulin to treat a low reading. S281

Shaking, sweating, hunger and confusion can fit hypoglycaemia, particularly with insulin or certain diabetes medicines, but do not diagnose every confused person this way. Focal deficits, chest symptoms, trauma or poor recovery require their own routes. Paediatric carbohydrate and rescue-product doses belong to the child's plan, not scaled adult amounts. A reading does not replace observation of consciousness and swallowing.

## Burns, fractures and head injury

Burn first actions: **Stop exposure safely, cool a thermal burn with cool running water for 20 minutes and remove nearby jewellery/clothing unless stuck.** Keep the rest of the person warm. Cover the cooled area loosely with a suitable clean non-adherent covering; cling film can be laid over, not wrapped around. Seek emergency assessment for large/deep burns, face involvement, breathing concerns, electrical or chemical injury. Do not use ice, butter, oils or creams, or burst blisters. Chemical/electrical hazards require isolation and specific instructions; a small skin mark does not prove minor injury. S283

Fracture first actions: **Keep still, support the injured part in its found position and seek care.** Emergency help is appropriate for open fracture, suspected neck/back/pelvis injury, major long-bone injury, shock, numbness or cold limb. Cover wounds without pushing bone back. Do not straighten, test weight bearing or massage. A swollen painful small-limb injury without these signs still needs assessment but may use supported transport under local advice. Ability to move does not exclude fracture. S284

Head injury first actions: **Call emergency services for unresponsiveness, inability to stay awake, seizure, new neurological problems or a major mechanism.** Minimise unnecessary movement, monitor breathing and follow the dispatcher. Vomiting, persistent headache, blood-thinner use or concerning symptoms needs urgent advice even if initially talking normally. After professional minor-injury assessment, follow discharge instructions and adult supervision advice. Do not use a photo, pupil check or conversation to clear injury, or return to sport while symptomatic. S285 S268

## Possible sepsis and rapid deterioration

First actions: **Call emergency services for infection-like illness with confusion, severe/very fast breathing, blue/pale/blotchy appearance, inability to wake normally or rapid deterioration.** Explain infection concern, trajectory, immune suppression or recent pregnancy. Observe breathing and responsiveness. Do not wait for fever, rash, pressure or a home score to confirm sepsis. S286

Children may show abnormal responsiveness, poor feeding, weak/high-pitched cry or respiratory distress; young-infant fever thresholds also need prompt assessment. Very low temperature matters, and absence of fever does not establish safety. A non-blanching rash is concerning but not required. Ordinary mild infection with normal responsiveness and breathing calls for appropriate observation/advice and escalation for worsening; do not label fever alone sepsis. Sepsis needs clinical hospital assessment and treatment, not home antibiotic selection. S286

## Water rescue and drowning

First actions: **Call emergency services and alert a lifeguard; do not enter dangerous water if untrained.** From a safe position, offer a flotation aid or reach with a rescue aid without risking being pulled in. Once safely out, check responsiveness/breathing and start resuscitation if unresponsive and breathing abnormally. Rescue breaths are important in drowning; use local training and dispatcher guidance. ERC/RCUK 2025 gives five initial rescue breaths followed by standard CPR. If unable to give breaths, start compressions and receive guidance. Dry the chest quickly for AED pads; do not delay CPR to dry the entire person. S268

Do not waste time attempting to “squeeze water out” with abdominal thrusts, hanging or chest pressure instead of CPR. Keep a person who is breathing warm and observed; persistent cough, breathlessness, confusion or deterioration after submersion needs urgent assessment. Do not send someone back swimming because they seem briefly better. The lay default is safe rescue, early call and breathing support; in-water breaths belong to trained rescuers with flotation, not a chat instruction for an untrained bystander. S268

## Heat illness and hypothermia

For heat exposure with confusion, poor coordination, seizure, collapse or severe breathing problems: **Call emergency services now, move to shade/cool surroundings and begin active cooling immediately.** Remove excess clothing; use available cool water with fanning or cool wet coverings while waiting. Do not wait for a thermometer or dry skin: suspected heat stroke is an emergency even if sweating. Do not force drinks with impaired consciousness. Trained event teams may use supervised cold-water immersion; an unresponsive person must not be left in a bath where their airway is at risk. S295 S293

A fully alert person with milder heat exhaustion can rest somewhere cool, cool the skin and drink water or appropriate rehydration fluid if swallowing safely. Stay with them. NHS expects improvement within 30 minutes; persistent illness requires escalation, but new confusion/collapse means call immediately rather than waiting for that interval. This is recognition, not a temperature-only diagnosis. S295

For suspected hypothermia after cold exposure, especially confusion, slurred speech, slow breathing or a floppy unusually sleepy infant: **Get emergency medical help, move gently to shelter and prevent further cooling.** Remove wet clothing carefully, insulate with dry coverings including the head and protect from cold ground/wind. Monitor breathing; use the dispatcher/CPR pathway for unresponsiveness and abnormal breathing. A warm non-alcoholic drink is only for someone fully awake and swallowing safely. Do not use a hot bath, direct intense heat, alcohol or limb rubbing; do not force activity. Do not assume absent shivering means recovery. S296

## Help with the person's asthma rescue plan

For severe breathing difficulty, inability to speak normally, worsening symptoms, exhaustion or poor response: **Call emergency services now. Sit upright and help the person use their own prescribed reliever under their asthma action plan and device instructions.** Use a spacer if appropriate for that inhaler and available. Stay, monitor breathing and follow the dispatcher; start CPR for unresponsiveness with abnormal breathing. Do not delay calling for a peak-flow measurement or send them to drive for help. S297

The rescue inhaler may be a short-acting bronchodilator or an AIR/MART combination product. They are not interchangeable: verify the actual inhaler and plan, rather than assuming every blue-looking device or preventer has the same rescue schedule. Do not create a universal puff count, prescribe oral steroids or borrow another person's medicines. If breathing symptoms follow an allergen with throat swelling/faintness, use the anaphylaxis route; the inhaler does not replace adrenaline. Recovery from an attack still warrants prompt clinical review and plan check. S297 S177

## Explanation after emergency actions

Once calling and immediate care are underway, explain only what helps the next decision. Request a brief update on consciousness/breathing, actions taken and dispatcher instructions. Do not run a competing long interview. Say “Several causes are possible, but this combination needs emergency assessment.” Onset times, medicines and exposures help responders; unnecessary identifiers do not help public educational content.

This module supports verified lay first aid and existing prescribed rescue plans. It does not authorise independent injections, IV fluids, oxygen titration, intubation, spine manipulation or prescription regimens. Arrange practical accredited first-aid education outside an emergency. Explain evidence and regional differences during preparation, not beside an unresponsive person. Sources are guidance/product material inspected in relevant sections, not project trials or validation of the assistant as an emergency service.
