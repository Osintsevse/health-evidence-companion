# Reference architecture for public medical knowledge

## Purpose and boundary

This packet describes how to build and maintain a public, source-linked medical knowledge library that can support a conversational assistant. It is a reference architecture and research map. It is not a clinical guideline, clinical validation report, triage algorithm, diagnostic model, or authorization to deploy patient-specific clinical decision support. The design goal is narrower: help a reader find well-bounded explanations and prepare for a conversation with a qualified clinician, while making evidence status, source scope, uncertainty, jurisdiction, and change history visible.

The architecture should preserve the distinction among three jobs: (1) teaching foundational concepts, (2) explaining evidence and guideline options for a defined population and setting, and (3) interpreting an individual person's situation. A foundation text can explain a mechanism but cannot establish that an intervention improves an outcome. A professional curriculum describes educational expectations but cannot substitute for clinical evidence. A guideline provides bounded recommendations in a context and version; it is not a universal law. A regulator's approval or clearance is a product-specific legal status, not proof that every use is beneficial or that a professional society recommends it. The user-facing system should expose these distinctions in the retrieved answer rather than compressing them into a single "best" source. S524 S530 S532

A large library is not validated merely because it is large, structured, frequently updated, or linked to respected organizations. A polished disclaimer cannot repair an unsupported treatment claim, a license violation, a broken safety branch, an out-of-scope population, or a system that turns uncertain observations into a diagnosis. Validation belongs to the complete product, its users, intended purpose, workflows, content, and updates. For conversational use, present the material as education and navigation; never imply a clinician has examined the person or that an automated output confirms or excludes a condition. A symptom label, image, screening result, laboratory flag, or score is a clue that may need professional interpretation, not a diagnosis by itself. S531 S534

## Build a map before expanding the library

A useful completeness map is multidimensional. It should let maintainers see which areas have reviewed explanations, which have current local guidance, and which remain unresearched. At minimum, index knowledge across: anatomy and organ systems; symptoms and diseases; medicines and therapeutic classes; diagnostic tests and imaging; procedures and devices; care settings; urgency and first aid; and life stages or populations. Add cross-cutting dimensions for pregnancy and postpartum, children, older adults, disability, mental health, prevention, rehabilitation, and social or access context where relevant. Use recognized terms and aliases where available, but keep the source's exact scope and plain-language labels. A body-system list alone misses medicines and tests; a disease catalogue alone hides life-stage and urgency gaps. Do not convert a map into a score that says the package is "complete." It is a work queue and gap register, not proof of safety or clinical coverage.

Represent a topic as linked, typed nodes rather than a pile of paragraphs. Illustrative types include: concept, symptom, condition, risk factor, medicine ingredient, exact product, formulation, route, test, procedure, device, intervention, life-stage, care setting, jurisdiction, guideline recommendation, evidence review, regulator status, and source. Edges should say what relation is asserted: "explains mechanism," "is considered in," "measures," "may mimic," "recommended by," "approved for," "contraindicated in," "superseded by," or "requires local verification." An edge is not free-floating: it needs a provenance pointer to the exact source and locator, the responsible reviewer, and a date. A positive association is not a recommendation; a trial registration is not an approval; approval is not a guideline recommendation; and a recommendation in one country does not automatically hold elsewhere.

Each topic page can begin with a compact scope card: what question it answers, who and where the source applies to, the date/version, the main outcomes at stake, important exclusions, and which questions remain unanswered. Keep mechanism, evidence, guideline recommendation, regulatory status, practical availability, and personal decision-making as separate fields. The WHO SMART/DAK model is a useful architectural analogue because it separates operational workflows, data needs, decision logic, indicators, and functional requirements. The WHO material also emphasizes that generic content needs country contextualization. It is an implementation pattern, not evidence that every DAK is effective or a justification to repurpose its algorithms as chat advice. S523 S525 S527

Curricula can help identify educational prerequisites and topic gaps. Compare broad outcomes, practical competencies, and continuing-development processes, but keep them in a curriculum layer, not the treatment evidence layer. GMC graduate outcomes and ACGME residency requirements describe expected learning and supervised development in their own jurisdictions; the GMC CPD page describes planning and evaluating professional learning. These are useful for a learning index, not proof that an online module teaches a user to practice medicine. A public learning route should label a resource as an introduction, curriculum standard, lecture, chapter, or professional competency. It must not suggest that reading it confers a license, hands-on competence, or clinical clearance. S536 S537 S538

## Evidence records and status over time

Use one evidence record for each consequential claim, not one source record per topic. A claim record should include a concise original statement, claim type, population, setting, intervention or exposure, comparator, outcome, certainty/limitations, source version, exact section/table/page, jurisdiction, retrieval date, reviewer, and links to related claims that agree or conflict. If the evidence is a trial or review, note design and search/cut-off date. If the claim comes from a guideline, identify the specific recommendation, its strength or certainty if stated, the population and exceptions, and the local version. Preserve disagreement between high-quality sources rather than choosing the newest document by timestamp alone. A correction or retraction must be connected to every claim it affects.

Clinical status should be explicit and temporal. Useful states include: established practice supported by a current applicable guideline; a current guideline option whose role depends on individual circumstances; a recently issued or materially changed guideline recommendation awaiting local adoption; a regulator-authorized product for a precisely named indication and market; investigational intervention or ongoing trial; evidence suggesting no benefit or a safety concern; discontinued, withdrawn, recalled, or unavailable item; and unknown or not yet reviewed. These states are not an efficacy ranking. "New" means new to a source or market, not effective. A clinical-trial registry entry shows a study exists, not that it worked. A product's authorization, indication, formulation, route, age range, and geography must match the discussion. Keep a regulator-status claim linked to the relevant database and label; separately link the guideline's treatment recommendation and evidence basis. Explain alternatives as topics for clinician discussion rather than choosing or prescribing for a person.

The source catalogue is an index, not a corpus permission slip. For every source, record who publishes it, edition/version, publication and update date, region, relevant section locator, how deeply it was read, URL, corrections or replacements, intended use, limitations, and rights. Reading depth should use literal values such as full relevant section read, selected sections read, abstract only, official landing page only, TOC/catalogue only, search-index excerpt, blocked, or rights metadata only. Never say "reviewed the book" when you read only its abstract or table of contents. Attach rights at the work and, where necessary, chapter/asset level. Public access, a free PDF, or inclusion in a biomedical bookshelf does not establish reuse rights. NLM Bookshelf explicitly distinguishes its full archive from a separate open-access subset; it is necessary to inspect the exact title's statement and any third-party credits. Even a Creative Commons notice may have exceptions. S539 S540

Open resources are helpful only when the exact license fits the intended use. Open RN's second-edition Nursing Pharmacology page states CC BY 4.0, while also noting that it is not a drug-reference book and that underlying adapted content may require attribution checks. The chapter text was not read for this packet, so this is only a candidate learning route. By contrast, the OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology 2e rights notice states CC BY-NC-SA and expressly restricts LLM training or ingestion without prior written permission; only that rights metadata is considered here. The ESC's licensing page similarly says explicit permission is required for AI/CDS applications using guideline content. These examples show why each source needs a rights decision before download, indexing, transformation, AI ingestion, or redistribution. Keep the copyright status separate from the scientific merit. When rights are uncertain, link to the source and write an independent explanation grounded in evidence that may lawfully support it; do not copy a protected passage into embeddings or a generated knowledge card. This is an operational control, not a legal opinion. S84 S541

## Retrieval that exposes provenance

Retrieval should be progressive. First resolve the user's question to a topic and task type: basic explanation, evidence comparison, test/result education, treatment-choice preparation, urgency concern, or source search. If the available context cannot resolve age group, pregnancy, jurisdiction, product/route, or relevant time course, either ask a small number of high-value questions or explain how the answer changes with that missing context. Do not collect context merely because it is available. A source that only matches the organ name but not population or jurisdiction is not an adequate retrieval result.

Second, retrieve a small evidence bundle: the most applicable current official guideline or local regulator/label for practice and product status; a high-quality evidence review or pivotal study for benefit/harms and uncertainty; and a foundational source only if mechanism or terminology needs explanation. Include a competing credible recommendation if one exists. For a patient-facing explanation, add a trusted plain-language or shared-decision resource rather than returning a stack of specialist documents. Rank by applicability and quality before recency, then check corrections, withdrawals, and updates. Display citation links close to each material claim, with title, source organization, version/date, relevant section, population and jurisdiction, and a short reason the source applies. If the relevant full text was not read, say so and limit the claim to metadata that was inspected.

Third, generate a response that separates: established facts; reasonable options; uncertainty and disagreement; status/availability; and what question to take to a clinician. Use absolute effects when reliable and understandable, with denominator and follow-up, rather than presenting relative percentages alone. State harms and burdens alongside possible benefits. For treatment choices, explain common options and trade-offs to help someone prepare for a clinical discussion. Do not produce individualized prescriptions, start/stop/taper instructions, dose calculations, renal or pediatric dosing, or product substitutions from chat. Existing clinician-prescribed rescue plans and verified lay first-aid instructions belong to a distinct urgent-care pathway with strict boundaries. A bare blood-pressure number, glucose number, risk score, or other isolated measurement must never trigger advice to lower/raise it or start insulin. A photograph may help describe a suitable visible feature, but image analysis must not assign a diagnosis, and obtaining a picture must never delay emergency help.

The existing navigation graph can remain the user's entry and cross-link layer. Add reference discovery as a compatible overlay: each existing destination can optionally declare its covered concepts, source keys, evidence status, jurisdiction, population and refresh trigger. Do not replace its route IDs or introduce a parallel general-purpose decision tree. When a new topic has no safe matching route, route to general education, source discovery, or qualified care rather than guessing an action. Keep route logic understandable to maintainers: trigger, minimum context, allowed actions, avoid list, escalation boundary, and source pointers. HL7 CQL/FHIR Clinical Reasoning and CDS Hooks show how clinical logic and workflow integration can be formally represented; they are engineering references for clinical systems, not a reason to implement executable scoring in this education-oriented library. ## Refresh and governance

A source update is an event to investigate, not an automatic content rewrite. Track triggers at the source-claim edge: publisher posts a new edition; a linked guideline is replaced or corrected; regulator label, approval, recall, or safety alert changes; an important randomized trial or systematic review appears; a major society makes a recommendation change; a safety signal or withdrawal occurs; local policy or availability changes; or a reviewer discovers contradiction, dead link, rights change, or population mismatch. Keep a named person accountable for checking a high-impact alert, and record what they inspected and what changed. NICE surveillance material illustrates event-based and scheduled review; an annual or quarterly queue is only a planning aid. No interval can guarantee freshness. High-consequence medicines, vaccines, devices, screening, reproductive care, and emergency advice need shorter checks and point-of-use confirmation against local sources. S535 S533

A refresh should compare the exact old and new version. Record the publication date and version, date checked, recommendation changes, population and setting differences, benefit/harms or certainty changes, regulator and product status, rights status, and downstream routes/evaluations affected. Changes can add a recommendation, narrow it, suspend it, supersede it, or leave it unchanged. A source's new web page date does not prove its clinical recommendation changed. Preserve the previous version, correction history, and reviewer rationale in the repository's normal provenance mechanism. Do not delete old evidence without a trace, and do not silently swap a source behind a stable citation token.

Treat support materials and code as separately licensed. The WHO TB repository provides a concrete warning: its README and LICENSE distinguish WHO-authored educational/DAK content under CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO from software under BSD terms. GitHub stars, a README claim, a package validator, a passing unit test, or a standards logo do not establish clinical effectiveness. Public AI skill and benchmark repositories are discovery leads: inspect their README, per-file license, scope, source dates, review process, evaluation definitions, and provenance. A recent skills repository can model folder layout for skill instructions, references, examples, and evaluation cases while still having little independent maintenance or clinical validation. An aggregated benchmark can contain mixed licenses and real clinical vignettes; its own top-level license may not cover the data. Use such projects to learn about transparent governance and evaluation design, not to import unscreened patient-like cases or repeat their clinical claims. S527 S542 S543

## Evaluation without pretending to validate care

Use synthetic, visibly fictional fixtures that test behavior across turns, not a test set of real patients. Cover missing context; contradictory sources; age, pregnancy, jurisdiction or product changes; a newly reported study; source retraction or product withdrawal; inaccessible source sections; unsupported diagnosis requests; urgent warning signs; a prompt to calculate a dose; and a user-provided screenshot or isolated number. Expected behavior should assess whether the assistant asks only for context that changes the answer, marks unknowns, uses the correct dated source, differentiates an approved product from a recommendation, preserves an urgent escalation, refuses unsupported patient-specific calculations, and explains the evidence in plain language. Each fixture should point to the relevant source keys and an explicit behavioral criterion. Keep gold checks focused on process and source use; avoid encoding an unreviewed clinical pathway in an answer key.

Evaluate citation entailment separately from citation presence. For every material sentence, a reviewer should confirm that the source passage actually supports it, matches the population and region, is current for the stated use, and has a compatible rights record. Test answer robustness to changed facts and sources: if jurisdiction changes, the recommendation wording must change or state that local verification is needed; if a supporting source is withdrawn, the answer must stop citing it; if two credible guidelines disagree, the response must show the disagreement. Also test retrieval misses and tool failures. A failure to fetch a source must not be represented as a source review. Monitor quality after release and after each content or retrieval change, but use synthetic cases and approved quality processes rather than collecting private user messages, health histories, or genetic records as a public training/evaluation set.

The FDA's 2026 CDS page and transparency principles are regulatory references for intended use, user context, and communicating output limits; they do not classify this packet's intended product or make it safe. AHRQ's diagnostic-team material emphasizes clear human roles and the clinician's responsibility when a clinical AI is used; its use here is a governance analogy, not endorsement of any conversational system. If a future feature crosses from education and reference navigation into individualized diagnosis, triage, treatment, or calculated dosing, this architecture alone is insufficient. It requires a separate product-intent analysis, applicable regulatory assessment, qualified clinical governance, usability work, representative validation, post-deployment monitoring, and update control. S532 S534 S531

The deliverable of a well-maintained reference library is therefore an inspectable map: what it covers, what it does not cover, which sources and versions support each statement, how rights were reviewed, who checked consequential changes, and how the system behaves when information is missing or contradictory. The size of the map is secondary to its traceability, scope discipline, and demonstrated behavior under change.


# Learning-section and course-outline supplement

This supplement adds bounded inspection of two clearly identified Open RN nursing-pharmacology sections and an official CDC clinician-training outline. It is a navigation supplement, not a full curriculum audit or clinical recommendation. The summaries below are original and concise; no chapter passages, textbook activities, answer keys, CDC assessment items, or images have been copied.

## Section summaries

**Open RN Nursing Pharmacology-2e, sections 1.2 and 1.7.** Section 1.2 introduces pharmacokinetics through absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, then frames time and measurement as part of understanding drug movement. Section 1.7 shifts to pharmacodynamics and mechanisms at target sites, including receptor agonist and antagonist vocabulary. Together, they provide a sensible foundation route from what the body does to a medicine to how a medicine can act. The sections are educational context only; they do not establish whether a specific product works, is appropriate, or is approved for an individual. S544 S547

**CDC, Core Curriculum on Tuberculosis, Chapter 1 selected material.** I read the chapter objectives and introduction and selected pages on transmission and pathogenesis. The chapter structure leads from objectives to transmission, pathogenesis, drug resistance, classification, and a knowledge check. The reviewed material frames TB transmission as influenced by the infectious source, exposure, susceptibility, and environment. This is a US clinician reference from 2021. Its introductory note says the manual is not a substitute for specific guidelines; I did not inspect later treatment chapters for clinical recommendations. S545

**CDC continuing-education activity SS4444R.** The public outline lists seven objectives spanning TB spread/pathogenesis, testing, diagnosis, latent and active TB treatment, infection control, and public/private health-sector roles. It names clinicians and nurses as its audience and expects medical vocabulary and practice experience. The page records an expiration date of March 19, 2025, so it is useful as an example of objective mapping, but not a current source of CE credit. I did not complete the course or its post-assessment. S546

## Rights and reading record

The Open RN publisher pages state that the 2023 second edition is CC BY 4.0 except where a page says otherwise. The inspected pages provide separate credits for figures: section 1.2 identifies its image as CC BY 4.0 and notes a derivative source from the public-domain NIH/NIGMS work *Medicines by Design*; section 1.7 credits its figure to Dominic Slausen/CVTC under CC BY 4.0. The book preface also lists adapted resources with CC BY-NC-SA and CC BY-SA terms. A book-level CC BY notice therefore does not remove the need to inspect and attribute each figure or adapted component. If content is reused, preserve attribution, identify changes, and avoid implying publisher or government endorsement. S544 S547

The exact CDC Stacks record for the seventh-edition curriculum marks the work as Public Domain. CDC's general materials policy adds conditions for reproducing CDC material, including attribution, an unambiguous non-endorsement notice, and avoiding substantive changes; it also warns that CDC sites can contain separately protected third-party content. I only linked the work and wrote original summaries. No logos, figures, tables, or course assessment questions are reused. Copyright rules can differ by jurisdiction, so this record is not legal advice. S545 S546

## Navigation use and gaps

Add these as learning routes in a foundations/reference index, not as standalone medical advice. A medicine question that uses unfamiliar terminology can point to Open RN 1.2 and 1.7, labelled “nursing education, US edition, foundational concepts.” A question about TB education can route to the CDC curriculum's chapter list, label the 2021 edition and link readers to the current CDC TB clinical guidance for practice decisions. The CE outline can show how training objectives connect a condition to testing, diagnosis, infection control, and public-health roles; its expired status should remain visible wherever it appears. Neither the course outline nor a learner's completion status should be treated as proof of clinical competence.

These sources expose useful architecture features: prerequisite concepts, explicitly named learning objectives, a chapter sequence, an audience and experience assumption, a source edition, a rights record, and an expiry state. They also show a gap in the current evidence map: curriculum discovery should track which concepts have actually been read at section level, not just which books or institutions have been found. A future route may add verified local guideline links and current product labels, but that task requires a separate clinical source review. The selected pharmacology sections cannot substantiate drug efficacy, interactions, contraindications, or dosing; the selected TB chapter cannot establish current testing or treatment advice. The broader textbooks, CDC manual, assessments, clinical guidelines, and international curricula have not been fully reviewed. This is a small navigational sample, not a completed pharmacology, infectious-disease, or clinician-education curriculum.

The foundational section summaries adapt only selected concepts from *Nursing Pharmacology-2e*, edited by Kimberly Ernstmeyer and Elizabeth Christman (2023), under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). They are newly written summaries with no figures or activities. The source is introductory nursing education with simplified explanations; it is not a quantitative pharmacology model or an exact medicine reference.
