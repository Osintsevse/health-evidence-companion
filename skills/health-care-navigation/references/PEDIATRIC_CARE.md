# Well-child care from birth through 59 months

Checked 2026-10-09. This module supports caregiver questions about children from birth through 4 years 11 months. It provides education, concern recognition and preparation for local pediatric care. It does not diagnose developmental conditions, prescribe pediatric treatment or issue clearance. A brief response can say: "This is general information; your child's clinician can assess the concern in context." Give urgent action before this sentence when necessary.

## Newborn care and screening: a concrete local plan

For the first days, ask age in days, gestational age, place of birth, whether the baby is home or receiving neonatal care, the discharge plan and the specific concern. A discharge examination, a screening sample and a scheduled home contact are different events. Help the caregiver identify who owns each follow-up and how to reach them. Do not interpret "everything was normal" as proof that all screening results are complete. Ask which examination or result the statement refers to. Current symptoms override a reassuring discharge record.

Use the maternity team's actual discharge and community-contact arrangements. Ask which service will review feeding and wellbeing after the baby goes home, when the next contact is planned, and how to obtain help sooner for a concern. Do not impose a universal post-transfer deadline. The already cited England newborn-examination programme has its own timing and professional roles; it does not establish the timing of every community visit. Keep examination, feeding support and pending screening follow-up as separate actions with named owners. S593

Prepare the next contact with three questions: "What has been completed? What is still outstanding? Who will contact us, and by when?" Ask for a written plan when the family moves between services. Preserve both sample date and result date. A preterm baby discharged from neonatal care may have additional hearing, retinal, nutritional or other follow-up determined by that team. Do not cancel these because the routine newborn screens passed. The head/fontanel and head-circumference module supplies the deeper skull assessment pathway; this module does not set fontanel closure deadlines or instruct palpation.

The NHS newborn physical examination example is within 72 hours and repeated at 6-8 weeks. A trained midwife, nurse or doctor checks the baby overall, with specific assessment of eyes, heart, hips and testes when applicable. Eye assessment uses an appropriate light examination; cardiac assessment includes listening and pulses; hips are examined by a trained professional. A concerning finding can prompt specialist tests. Results are usually discussed immediately and recorded. This examination does not mean every newborn needs hip ultrasound or genetic testing. Do not teach a caregiver to perform hip maneuvers at home or declare a red reflex normal from a photograph. The second examination matters because some findings emerge later. S593

NHS bloodspot screening normally takes place on day 5: a professional obtains a few heel-blood drops on a laboratory card. The caregiver can feed, cuddle and comfort the baby. The result can mean no condition suspected, a carrier finding or a condition suspected; a positive screen requires the programme's confirmatory assessment, not a chat diagnosis. A repeat can be needed for sample or clinical reasons. NHS advises expecting results by 6 weeks and contacting the service if they have not arrived. Panels vary by country and even current local implementation: the inspected NHS page describes SMA testing only in some parts of England. Do not promise that a heel-prick excludes every genetic, metabolic or developmental condition. S594

England's professional repeat-bloodspot chapter, updated 30 September 2026, specifies an additional congenital-hypothyroidism screen for babies born before 32 completed weeks at 28 days or discharge home, whichever is sooner, in addition to the routine sample. Transfusion can also require repeat sampling at least 3 clear days after the last transfusion for selected conditions. These are examples of why neonatal teams coordinate the plan, not a family-administered protocol. Ask the team which repeat is needed, its reason, timing and result contact. Borderline or incomplete results must not be converted into "negative". After transfer or moving country, notify the responsible screening service of outstanding samples. S595

For NHS newborn hearing screening, a soft earpiece and gentle clicks assess a settled or sleeping baby. An unclear response can lead to repeat screening or a different test; it is not proof of deafness. The inspected NHS page describes specialist audiology within 4 weeks of the initial screen when a clear result is absent in either ear. More detailed testing records responses through small sensors and headphones. A clear newborn result does not exclude all hearing loss or later-onset difficulty. Ask whether both ears were completed and whether an audiology appointment has actually been arranged. S596

US CDC describes critical-congenital-heart-defect screening using pulse oximetry at 24 hours or later, or as late as possible before an earlier discharge. Skin sensors estimate blood oxygen; the screen supplements history and examination. Passing cannot exclude every serious heart defect. Failing can reflect a heart defect or another cause of low oxygen and requires the service's further evaluation, which may include repeat screening or echocardiography. This is a US programme example: do not claim every UK, Serbian or Russian baby routinely receives this test. A home wearable or isolated reading is not equivalent to a newborn screening protocol. New symptoms require assessment even after a passed screen. S597

Routine cord care should be simple and localized. NHS bathing advice says to clean around the cord when soiled with urine or stool, using warm water and fresh cotton wool and then gently drying. Do not pull the stump or prescribe antiseptic products from a photo; follow a neonatal team's specific instructions. For redness spreading around the cord, discharge associated with illness or other new concerns, obtain clinical assessment through the acute infant pathway. Bathing does not require daily full immersion, and an adult must supervise continuously; prepare supplies before starting. S599

## Feeding and development before the first birthday

For a newborn feeding question, establish age, milk type, actual preparation method, the family's concern and any neonatal feeding plan. Offer practical support without judging the feeding choice. If the family needs help with breastfeeding, bottle feeding or mixed feeding, identify the local maternity or infant-feeding service and its agreed follow-up. Do not issue a compulsory remote volume schedule or infer adequate intake from feed duration alone. For powdered formula, the preparation safeguards below address hygiene and concentration; they do not assess milk transfer or replace a clinical feeding assessment. S600

Powdered infant formula is not sterile. The NHS preparation procedure uses clean hands and surfaces, cleaned/sterilized feeding equipment, freshly boiled drinking water still at least 70 C when mixed, measured water first and the exact product's specified powder amount. Cool the prepared feed before offering it; do not microwave. Make feeds individually as needed. Do not add extra water, extra powder, cereal or sugar: concentration changes can be harmful or nutritionally inadequate. A scoop from another product is not interchangeable. A preparation machine must actually deliver the required mixing temperature under the applicable instructions. Special medical formula and neonatal feeding require their professional/local product plan. S600

Around 6 months, complementary foods accompany breast milk or first infant formula. NHS readiness features involve stable head/sitting position, coordinated hand-mouth use and swallowing; waking at night or chewing fists alone does not establish readiness. Offer small amounts and progress textures, including mashed/lumpy and safe finger foods, according to ability. Milk remains important through infancy. Cows' milk can be used in food from around 6 months but is not the main drink before 12 months. Iron-containing foods and variety matter; avoid replacing milk feeds with water, juice or low-energy food. Prematurity, swallowing problems or special nutrition needs require an individualized team plan. S601

NHS advises introducing potential allergenic foods around 6 months, individually in small quantities so reactions can be recognized, then continuing tolerated foods in the usual diet. Use safe forms of nuts and seeds; this does not authorize whole nuts or thick nut-butter lumps. Existing food allergy or eczema and relevant family allergy history warrant discussion with the clinician before introduction. Do not perform a home challenge after a suspected reaction or promise that early introduction prevents every allergy. Follow the existing allergy plan when one is prescribed. S602

Before 12 months, developmental discussion should remain continuous, not start only when a child walks. Ask about interaction, response to voices, play, visual attention, movement and progress over time using the applicable local age-specific resources. Avoid turning a brief account into an exact developmental age. Prematurity correction and regression safeguards below also apply during infancy. A caregiver can support ordinary responsive conversation and safe floor play without buying a diagnostic app or forcing advanced motor practice. Use the newborn and later hearing/vision pathways when sensory concern changes the interpretation.

## Selected infant developmental observations at 2, 4, 6 and 9 months

These original prompts select a few examples from the directly inspected CDC age pages. They support observation and a clinical conversation, not a diagnosis, complete checklist, screening score or home motor examination. CDC's milestones describe abilities attained by most children, not guarantees about an individual child. Use corrected developmental age for prematurity as discussed below. Ask about progression and lost skills; a missed example or persistent concern merits discussion with the child's clinician, and regression must not be dismissed because another ability is present.

- At 2 months, discuss engagement with faces and voices, emerging non-crying sounds, and movement of both arms and legs. A social smile during interaction and briefly lifting the head during awake tummy play are useful caregiver examples. Offer calm face-to-face talking and respond to the infant's sounds, taking a break when they show fatigue. Do not expose the baby to loud noises to prove normal hearing or place them prone to sleep. S605
- At 4 months, discuss increasing head stability when held, vocal exchanges with a caregiver, and interest in hands or reachable toys. Examples include turning toward a familiar voice, holding a toy placed in the hand, and pushing through the forearms during awake tummy play. Provide safe floor-play opportunities rather than compulsory positioning exercises. A video of one good performance cannot exclude a concern about inconsistent responses or loss of head control. S606
- At 6 months, discuss laughter and recognition of familiar people, turn-taking sounds, reaching for an appealing toy, and changing positions. Selected examples include rolling from tummy to back and sitting supported through the hands. These observations are distinct from complementary-food readiness and do not mean every six-month-old can sit securely alone. Support exploration with safe objects; stop when tired or distressed rather than drilling a milestone. S607
- At 9 months, discuss varied babbling, response to name, interest in games, finding an object that fell out of sight, and sitting or transferring objects between hands. Selected CDC examples include getting into sitting and sitting without support. Ask about the whole communication and movement pattern rather than treating crawling style or one name-response attempt as a diagnosis. The US AAP nine-month formal screening recommendation is separate from these surveillance observations. S608 S579

## Infant sleep is not head-shape therapy

WHO describes sleep totals including naps of 14-17 hours at 0-3 months and 12-16 hours at 4-11 months. Infant screen time is not recommended. Interactive floor play is encouraged; for infants not yet mobile, supervised awake tummy time contributes to the recommended 30 minutes spread through the day. These public-health totals are descriptive guides, not an order to keep a sleepy or ill infant awake. Short comfortable opportunities can build up with the family's circumstances. S582

For sleep, place the infant on their back on a firm, flat surface in a separate clear cot, in the caregiver's room for at least the first 6 months. Do not add pillows, nests, bumpers, loose objects or positioning devices to treat a flat head or fontanel concern. Prone positioning is for awake supervised play, not sleep treatment. Avoid sofa/armchair sleeping with an infant and smoke exposure; bed sharing is especially unsafe with prematurity, low birthweight, smoking, alcohol or sedating substances. Premature babies need the neonatal team's safe-sleep advice; NHS describes continuing safer-sleep advice for a year from the due date. A head-shape concern belongs to the head/skull assessment pathway while sleep safety continues. S603

## Start with the caregiver's actual question

Ask the child's age in completed months, caregiver relationship, country of care and the main question. For a developmental concern, ask what the child currently does, what changed, when it changed, whether any skill was lost, and whether there are hearing or vision concerns. Ask gestational age at birth only when prematurity affects interpretation. For food questions, ask representative intake, known allergies, texture skills and any swallowing difficulty. For growth, ask dated measurements, units, method and chart already used. These are operational safeguards: do not request a full family dossier before answering a simple toothpaste or grape-preparation question.

Distinguish a caregiver report, a recorded measurement, a clinician's finding and an interpretation. "The nursery says he rarely talks" and "he uses phrases at home" can both be true. Ask about settings, languages and examples rather than selecting whichever account fits a diagnosis. Do not assume ancestry, genotype, family structure, developmental ability or diet from appearance or language. A calendar invitation for a routine visit is not a reason to postpone assessment of a new concern.

Explain the next useful step and what would change it. An answer about routine development should identify the relevant age range, offer one observation or supportive activity, and say when to contact the pediatric service. Avoid turning all seven age checkpoints below into a compulsory home examination. Caregiver concern deserves attention even when a checklist appears reassuring. Conversely, an isolated missed example cannot establish a disorder. S579

## Developmental surveillance, screening and assessment

Surveillance is continuing observation and discussion of progress in movement, communication, play, learning and social interaction. Formal screening uses validated tools with their intended age range, administration and follow-up. Diagnostic assessment is a further clinical process. A milestone page, video, score or chat interview cannot be substituted for that process. CDC describes AAP's US general developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months and autism screening at 18 and 24 months; concerns can require screening at other times, and a missed screen can be discussed at the next visit. These are US recommendations, not a universal Serbian or Russian visit calendar. S579

The age prompts below are original conversation aids using a few selected examples. They are not a copied questionnaire, scored instrument, complete list of milestones or diagnostic threshold. A child can show different abilities across settings. Let the caregiver describe ordinary play rather than repeatedly testing performance. For an age between listed checkpoints, review progress from the preceding stage and the next expected skills; do not pretend that every ability becomes due on one exact day.

- At 12 months, discuss social games, communication with gestures, exploration and supported mobility. Examples include waving, using a familiar name for a caregiver, putting an object into a container, pulling to stand and moving along furniture. Independent walking is not the only relevant observation at the first birthday. Ask what opportunities the child has to move safely and interact with an adult. S572
- At 15 months, discuss attempts at meaningful words beyond caregiver names, pointing to request help, using objects in familiar ways, and emerging independent steps. Shared attention matters alongside movement: ask whether the child brings or shows interesting things. Offer playful naming during routines, with time for the child to respond, instead of a vocabulary drill. S573
- At 18 months, discuss independent walking, pointing to share an interest, emerging words, simple instructions and participation in daily routines. Ask about comprehension as well as speech. Scribbling or trying a spoon can provide examples of hand use. A caregiver who notices loss of pointing or communication needs assessment rather than reassurance based on walking alone. S574
- At 24 months, discuss combining words, using gestures, running, ball play and using a spoon. Observe how the child combines actions during play and communicates needs. These examples are useful for an appointment description, not a rule that one phrase proves typical overall development. A communication concern can justify evaluation even if motor skills appear strong. S575
- At 30 months, discuss increasing language, pretend play, simple problem solving, following two linked instructions and emerging jumping. CDC's examples include approximately 50 words and combinations containing an action word, but do not invent an exact vocabulary count from a short conversation. Ask whether a formal developmental screen was offered in the applicable local pathway. S576
- At 36 months, discuss reciprocal conversation, how readily others understand speech, joining children's play and increasing dressing or eating independence. A child who speaks more at home than nursery still needs a setting-sensitive account. Support shared stories and play with peers without treating sociability, separation behavior or pronunciation as a standalone diagnosis. S577
- At 48 months, discuss sentences, describing something that happened, pretend roles, drawing and supervised practical tasks. Examples include catching a large ball and handling clothing fasteners. Children aged 49-59 months remain within this module: review continuing progress and preparation for local preschool or school assessment, without silently applying the five-year milestone list early. S578

Prematurity changes developmental comparison in early childhood. AAP caregiver guidance supports corrected developmental age during the first two years, calculated from chronological age minus the weeks born before 40 weeks' gestation. Use recorded gestational age and actual dates, not an assumed four-week month or an estimated birth story. Explain chronological and corrected ages separately. Correction does not dismiss regression, persistent concerns or a neonatal follow-up plan. Beyond two years, ask the treating team how continuing needs will be assessed rather than automatically extending a correction indefinitely. Do not use corrected developmental age as a vaccine schedule calculation. S587

Loss of acquired skills, multiple concerns or a caregiver's persistent worry should prompt pediatric discussion and appropriate assessment. Do not tell a family to wait for a birthday, a nursery place or an autism diagnosis before asking about support. Local early-intervention, child-development and preschool services differ; explain the route only after identifying jurisdiction. The US distinction between early intervention below three and school-system services from three is an example, not a global eligibility rule. A reassuring screening result does not erase new information. If neurological deterioration is sudden or accompanied by acute illness, use the acute pediatric pathway rather than a routine developmental appointment. S579

## Growth: measurement before labels

WHO Child Growth Standards include charts through five years. This does not mean every country uses the same chart at every age. CDC recommends WHO charts below age two in US care and CDC charts from two onwards. Preserve chart identity when comparing records across countries. Do not assign a percentile without valid age, measurements, units and the chart's required sex information; do not infer that information from a name or photograph. Adult BMI thresholds are inappropriate for these children. S581 S580

Below two, measurements generally use recumbent length; at two, standing height and growth indicators may change. Standing height is shorter than lying length, and a chart transition itself can alter percentile classification. Review serial accurate measurements and clinical context before interpreting a change. One home-scale value or a height marked on a wall is not a growth diagnosis. If a measurement appears inconsistent, suggest a proper repeat at the pediatric service rather than changing calories to chase a percentile. S580

Operationally, a useful growth question is: "Were both measurements taken the same way, and what does the clinician see in the trend?" Preserve uncertainty when dates or units conflict. Explain that growth review can involve feeding, symptoms and relevant history; it is not permission to request every possible blood test or a consumer genetic panel. A clinician should choose investigations for a specific question. Where intake, swallowing or growth is concerning, arrange pediatric assessment and suitably qualified dietetic support instead of a remote weight-loss plan.

## Feeding from one to four years

At 12-23 months, energy and fat remain important for growth. Offer varied familiar foods with appropriate textures, including sources of protein, iron and calcium. NHS guidance supports full-fat dairy foods up to two and warns that a diet relying exclusively on high-fibre starches can fill an under-two child before sufficient energy is eaten. At 24-59 months, lower-fat dairy foods can be introduced gradually when eating and growth are adequate; this is not permission to impose an adult fat-restriction diet. Milk can form part of the pattern after one, but evaluate it alongside food, breastfeeding if continued, and actual intake. S583

Iron-rich options include meat, fish, eggs, pulses and appropriately fortified foods. A vegetarian pattern requires attention to adequacy, not a presumption of deficiency. A dairy alternative's name alone does not establish its protein, energy or calcium equivalence. Check local labels and obtain professional advice when replacing milk for allergy or restricted intake. NHS advises against rice drinks below five and skimmed or 1% milk as the main drink below five. These are UK educational recommendations; individual feeding needs and local products still matter. S583

Selective eating is common, but the answer should first establish what is eaten across several days, whether growth is satisfactory and whether eating is painful or unsafe. Offer calm meals, small portions, repeated low-pressure opportunities and eating together. Plan meals and snacks so constant grazing does not replace meals. Respect refusal; do not force, bargain with sweets or compare the child harshly with another child. A practical experiment might be a small familiar meal with one optional new food, reviewed for comfort and participation rather than a clean plate. S584

Avoid routine adult calorie deficits, fasting, ketogenic restriction or "detox" plans for a healthy preschool child. This module does not determine an individual energy target from a photo or a single day. Supplements require an actual life-stage indication and local product review; do not replace adequate feeding with megavitamins or convert an adult supplement dose into a child dose. Unexplained weight loss, a very narrow deteriorating intake, painful swallowing, repeated choking or inability to eat deserves clinical assessment. State what is known and refer rather than labeling all difficult feeding as behavior.

Food allergy involves an immune reaction; intolerance describes a different process and can cause digestive symptoms. Timing and symptoms help prepare an assessment but do not settle the diagnosis. Ask the suspected food, reproducibility and clinical plan. Avoid broad food exclusions or unsupported home allergy testing. Breathing difficulty, swelling affecting the airway, collapse or an abnormally unresponsive child after food requires emergency help and the existing prescribed rescue plan; do not conduct a food diary interview first. Arrange pediatric/allergy review for suspected non-emergency allergy, with nutritional support where exclusions threaten adequacy. S333

Choking prevention applies throughout 12-59 months, including 4 years 11 months. Seat the child upright, alert and supervised while eating. Adjust shape and texture: quarter grapes and other small round foods, soften firm foods as needed, remove bones and avoid sausage coin shapes. Whole nuts and popcorn are unsuitable below five; nut butter should be used as a spread or mixed into food, not given alone in a lump. Cutting is risk reduction, not a guarantee of safe swallowing. A child with swallowing difficulty needs an individualized professional plan. Active choking belongs to first aid, not this preventive discussion. S585

## Sleep, active play and screens

WHO distinguishes ages 1-2 from 3-4: both groups should accumulate 180 minutes of varied activity daily; ages 3-4 include 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous play. Sleep including naps is 11-14 hours at 1-2 and 10-13 hours at 3-4. Sedentary screens are not recommended at 1, and are limited to 1 hour at 2-4, less preferred. Avoid restraint over 1 hour at a time and prolonged sitting. S582

These are public-health ranges and totals, not a rigid training timetable or an instruction to exhaust the child. Spread enjoyable movement through the day and use quiet shared reading during sedentary time. Discuss family access, disability and what a realistic change would replace. A single short night or a necessary journey does not warrant blame. Persistent sleep problems with functional concerns should be discussed with the pediatric service; do not prescribe sedating medicines or supplements from a chat. Separate an educational screen-time goal from treating a diagnosed sleep or developmental condition.

## Teeth, hearing and vision

Brush from the first tooth and maintain adult help. NHS guidance specifies twice-daily fluoride brushing, including bedtime: a smear below three, then a pea-sized amount from three through six. Check the fluoride concentration on the actual tube. UK guidance uses at least 1,000 ppm below three, with a dentist able to advise a higher concentration; at three to six it describes 1,000-1,500 ppm. Supervise, prevent toothpaste eating, and encourage spitting without rinsing. A child who cannot spit needs careful help with the small age-appropriate amount, not an unsupported switch to fluoride-free toothpaste. S586

Arrange regular dental review according to local access and risk. NHS recommends first attendance when teeth appear or before twelve months; for an older child who has never attended, arrange a first visit now rather than describing a missed opportunity as irreversible. Tooth pain or swelling requires dental assessment. This module does not prescribe fluoride tablets, professional varnish or antibiotics. Discuss the local dentist's preventive plan and sugar exposure without shaming the caregiver. S586

Speech delay, unclear speech, difficulty following spoken directions or repeatedly increasing volume can be hearing concerns. A passed newborn hearing screen does not exclude a later problem. Ask for hearing assessment promptly when concern arises; a new developmental concern should not be explained away as disobedience. Hearing screening identifies possible difficulty, while failed screening needs fuller testing. Avoid loud home tests or claims that a video proves normal hearing. Hearing services and developmental support can proceed together. S588

Vision assessment is also more than looking at a photograph. AAPOS describes referral for misaligned eyes, persistent tearing/discharge or failed photoscreening in the 12-36-month group. Its US professional screening guidance distinguishes ages 36-47 months from 48-59 months and uses age-dependent acuity criteria. Explain that a trained screening service uses appropriate tests; do not reproduce an optotype chart or score home smartphone testing. Country preschool screening ages differ, so verify the local programme. Suspected vision problems deserve assessment even if the next school screen is months away. S589

## Toileting without pressure

Discuss practical abilities, comfort and opportunities to learn rather than imposing a single deadline. The current NHS Best Start guidance encourages practice around 18-30 months and explicitly says caregivers need not wait for readiness signs or the child to request a potty. This differs from older advice that required such signs. Support comfortable sitting, accessible clothing, foot support, routine opportunities and calm responses to accidents. Children with disabilities may need extra support. Daytime learning and nighttime dryness are different; do not punish wet nights. At three or four, ongoing difficulty can justify support without treating the child as at fault. S590

Check constipation before escalating training pressure. Hard painful stools, withholding and apparent loose soiling can accompany constipation; soiling is not proof of deliberate misbehavior. Persistent symptoms need pediatric review. NIDDK advises prompt medical attention when constipation occurs with blood, vomiting, constant abdominal pain, bloating or weight loss; do not simply add fibre and wait in these circumstances. This packet does not prescribe laxative doses, enemas or stop a medicine that may contribute. Bring symptom duration and existing treatment to the clinician. S591

## Safety and local preventive care

Revisit home hazards as mobility and climbing change. Keep medicines, cleaning products, nicotine liquids, button batteries and small magnets inaccessible; child-resistant packaging is not childproof. Secure blind cords and access to stairs and windows. Keep hot drinks and cooking hazards beyond reach. Direct adult supervision is essential around baths and other water; bath seats and another child are not substitutes. Explain prevention briefly, then route an actual ingestion, drowning incident or injury to the acute pathway. Do not ask for a photo to decide whether a suspected battery ingestion is safe. S592

For well visits and vaccination, identify the actual country, recorded antigens/products, administration dates and the family's current service. This is a documentation workflow, not an individual catch-up schedule. Preserve unknown dates and distinguish a recommended calendar entry from a documented dose. A vaccination professional should reconcile previous records, minimum intervals and local rules. Do not import a universal US schedule or repeat Serbia/Russia landmarks as current without reading the original calendar and amendments for the consequential question. The existing vaccination module is the country-navigation entry point; this packet adds no unverified national dates.

A routine visit agenda can be short: the main caregiver concern, growth trend, feeding or sleep difficulty, hearing/vision questions, oral health, toileting and vaccination records relevant to the appointment. Add tests only when a clinician identifies an indication or an applicable national screening programme. Do not propose a broad laboratory, allergy or genetic package as proof of a healthy child. A development checklist cannot confer an ASD diagnosis or rule it out. Support can be useful before a final diagnostic label.

## Vaccination literacy and a blank professional-review planner

Calendar versions and birth cohorts matter. The UKHSA schedule currently inspected is effective 1 July 2026 and updated 24 August 2026. Its publication hub records introduction of MMRV and a new 18 month appointment in 2026, and removal of the discontinued Hib/MenC product from the current table. This illustrates why an old copied calendar cannot simply be rolled forward. Use the current national original, amendments and local service instructions; a recent search date does not prove an older table remains applicable. Serbia/Russia current originals were not independently refreshed for this packet. S604

In the inspected UK example,8/12/16 week visits include the six-in-one antigens for diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, Hib and hepatitisB, with other vaccines at selected visits. Rotavirus is given orally; MenB, pneumococcal and combined childhood vaccines address different infections. At one year the current table lists pneumococcal, MenB and MMRV. At 18 months, six-in-one and MMRV apply to children born on/after 1 July 2024; older birth cohorts do not have that routine appointment. The 3 years 4 months visit also has cohort-dependent MMRV wording. BCG and extra hepatitisB birth-related protection are selective programmes, not a universal UK birth bundle. Check cohort footnotes and annual influenza guidance rather than infer eligibility from age alone. S604

These names describe antigens and programme purposes, not a prescribing order. Separate vaccine composition, documented dose, programme recommendation, contraindication and funding. The family's question may be "Which infection does this combined product cover?" rather than "Which dose should we give today?" Preserve the original product name alongside translated antigen names. In records transferred between countries, vaccination services should verify composition and validity before counting doses. Missing documentation is unknown, not proof of immunity or nonvaccination. Do not generate a personal catch-up timetable without complete records and the current local rules.

True contraindications differ from precautions and from common misconceptions. Severe allergic reaction to a prior dose or component can contraindicate the relevant vaccine; live vaccines require special review with severe immunodeficiency. Rotavirus has specific contraindications including severe combined immunodeficiency and prior intussusception. Moderate/severe acute illness is commonly a precaution; mild illness, antibiotics or ordinary non-vaccine allergy are not automatically reasons to miss vaccination. Prematurity is not itself a blanket contraindication, but some vaccine-specific circumstances differ. A vaccination professional must check the exact product and child context before administration; this is not a chat clearance. S110

A blank planning row can contain: country/programme and version; child's relevant age/birth cohort; antigen(s); product if known; documented date and evidence source; status (documented/unknown/professional review needed); question for service; planned appointment; responsible service; follow-up/result. Leave all personal fields empty in public knowledge. Do not prefill doses from memory. Separate "appointment planned" from "dose administered" and preserve corrections with provenance. If catch-up is needed, ask the service which doses remain valid, which age/interval constraints apply and how the plan will be documented. This original workflow helps a family prepare without becoming an automated eligibility engine.

## Privacy, follow-up and limits

Use text observations first. Photos and recordings are optional and cannot replace an examination; avoid identifying school uniforms, addresses or unnecessary intimate material. Never request toileting photos. Explanation does not authorize archiving or sharing.
