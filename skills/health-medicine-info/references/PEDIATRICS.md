# Acute illness and safety: birth through 59 months

Reviewed 2026-10-09. This module supports caregiver education, urgency recognition and preparation for examination from birth through age 4 years 11 months. Neonates, young infants, older infants, toddlers and preschool children have distinct pathways; this is not a complete textbook or validated remote triage tool. Establish chronological age, gestational age at birth when relevant and illness trajectory. Chronological age selects first-aid and product age categories; prematurity/corrected age informs relevant developmental or clinical assessment without silently changing an age cutoff. Developmental delay does not turn a child aged at least one year into an infant for choking care. Birth-room neonatal resuscitation is a separate professional workflow, not a home algorithm. After birth, an infant or child's collapse follows the appropriate existing lay EMERGENCY/dispatcher pathway.

## Start with the decision that matters now

For severe breathing difficulty, blue/grey colour, collapse, inability to wake normally, an ongoing first seizure or rapid deterioration, tell the caregiver to call local emergency services before asking questions. Help them give location, approximate age, breathing and responsiveness. Follow the existing EMERGENCY module for child resuscitation, choking, anaphylaxis and drowning. It already distinguishes 2025 UK/ERC and US/AHA sequences; do not merge them or repeat a competing CPR algorithm here. For a child who is unresponsive and breathing abnormally, calling and dispatcher-guided CPR take precedence over temperature measurement, medicine, photographs or transport planning.

For other symptoms, establish exact age in months, main concern, onset and direction of change, usual versus current alertness/activity, breathing, fluid tolerance and urine output. Ask about underlying heart/lung disease, immune suppression, recent injury or possible ingestion only when relevant. Check what has already been given and any written rescue or discharge plan. Use the caregiver's observation without treating omitted details as normal. A child may need examination even when a number, photo or earlier telephone assessment seemed reassuring.

Give one concrete next step with its reason and a short safety net. In stable mild illness, name what can be observed and when reassessment is needed. Emergency, urgent same-day assessment and planned review are navigation categories, not a diagnostic score. Use the current country: NHS 111/999 and US poison numbers are examples of those systems. Verify local services when time permits; an unknown location must not delay saying to call the local emergency number. Finish with a brief informational disclaimer after necessary action.

## Newborn and young-infant illness: lower threshold, even without fever

A newborn who feeds poorly, becomes unusually quiet/floppy, is hard to wake, breathes abnormally or feels unusually cold can be seriously ill without fever. Do not describe absence of fever as proof of wellness, attribute sustained feeding refusal to normal sleepiness or wait for all warning signs. NHS under-five guidance identifies low temperature below 36 C, weak/high-pitched cry and abnormal responsiveness as concerning. Clear deterioration means emergency action, not a ten-minute thermometer experiment. For an otherwise stable unexpected reading, device technique may be checked alongside urgent professional advice; do not let repeat measurements postpone care. A bulging fontanelle with illness needs urgent assessment; detailed skull/fontanelle development and observation belong to the separate fontanelle module. S565

In an infant younger than three months, a measured temperature at least 38 C requires prompt clinical assessment even if the baby initially appears comfortable. At three to six months, a temperature at least 39 C also needs prompt assessment; lower numbers do not establish safety. Older-infant/toddler thresholds must not be substituted for these bands. Recent vaccination is relevant context but cannot automatically dismiss fever or new poor responsiveness. Arrange urgent age-specific clinical assessment now; if in the UK, the latest under-five urgent-help page directs these young-infant temperature findings to emergency care. Use emergency services immediately for danger signs or rapid deterioration. Do not leave a stressed caregiver to compare source categories or reassure from the number alone. S548 S565

The acute question is whether the baby needs examination now, not whether every test is needed. Explain that the care team chooses investigations to answer the presenting problem and assesses whether observation or hospital treatment is needed. Do not issue a universal young-infant test bundle or a neonatal antibiotic protocol. For fever-associated seizures, the patient information describes possible blood, urine and lumbar-puncture tests, not a requirement for each in every child. A lumbar puncture collects cerebrospinal fluid for laboratory testing and is a professional hospital procedure; discuss its purpose, consent and clinician-selected indication without procedural coaching or promising it is always or never required. S554 S631

## Neonatal jaundice: time in hours and gestation matter

Yellow skin/eyes in the first 24 hours needs immediate assessment. Later suspected jaundice also warrants timely professional review, especially worsening yellowing, dark urine or pale stools. Poor feeding, difficulty waking, abnormal tone/movements, abnormal breathing or temperature makes the situation more urgent. Visible colour can be difficult to judge across skin tones and lighting; a photograph cannot estimate bilirubin or establish that treatment is unnecessary. The NHS describes serum or transcutaneous measurement, with further selected tests when an underlying problem is suspected. S566

Ask for the baby's age in hours, gestation at birth, measured bilirubin, units and collection time rather than comparing the result with an adult laboratory limit or giving one universal safe value. Archived federal patient information explains that bilirubin interpretation uses age in hours and other risk factors; this supplies background literacy, not a current treatment threshold or follow-up algorithm. Current patient information recommends review of jaundice that persists, worsens or is accompanied by dark urine or pale stools. The special-care page advises same-day contact when jaundice remains after two weeks; it also notes that premature babies may remain yellow longer, without making that permission to wait through concerning features. Medical phototherapy uses specified equipment and supervision; sunlight or a household lamp is not a substitute. S632 S630 S566

Continue feeding support while the care team assesses jaundice. Current federal patient-care information supports continuing breastfeeding for most affected newborns, with supplementation selected case by case by the clinical team. S633 NHS describes phototherapy when required, sometimes through an organized home-equipment service; this is not permission to construct home treatment. Rare severe cases may need transfusion or intravenous treatment in hospital. Ask what the measured result, units, collection time, gestation and follow-up plan were, preserving unknowns. A useful question for the care team is when the next measurement is due and which feeding or behavior changes require earlier contact. Do not assume discharge means no reassessment is needed. S566

## Infant feeding, reflux and age-appropriate hydration

Small effortless milk spit-ups in a comfortable, growing infant are common. Establish feeding pattern, growth trend and whether the event is truly effortless regurgitation versus forceful vomiting. Persistent distress, feeding refusal, poor gain/weight loss, new reflux after six months or persistence beyond a year deserves clinical review. Projectile vomiting, blood, swollen/tender abdomen or inability to retain feeds needs urgent assessment. Green/bilious vomiting follows the emergency vomiting route, overriding a previous reflux label. Do not ask for a photo to clear it or assume all crying represents acid disease. S568

Seek support with feeding technique; hold the awake baby upright during/after feeding as advised, then use flat back sleeping. Do not elevate the cot or recommend prone/side sleeping for reflux. Clinicians may consider thickening or selected reflux medicines and assess possible cow's-milk allergy; these decisions do not justify caregiver-initiated cereal additives, elimination diets or acid suppressants. Distinguish a suggested option from a prescribed plan and ask what problem the intervention is meant to improve. S568

Milk remains central during the first year. Fully breastfed babies generally do not need extra water before complementary foods; offer more breastfeeds in hot weather rather than replacing milk. NHS has a limited hot-weather small-sip cooled-boiled-water option for formula-fed infants under six months, which is not universal water supplementation or treatment for an ill newborn. Confirm local clinician/feeding advice, maintain correct formula preparation and never dilute feeds. From around six months, small water sips with complementary meals are appropriate; do not import toddler 'drink plenty of water' instructions into an infant. Poor feeding or dehydration requires age-specific assessment rather than a home water quota. S569

## Procedures, vital observations and laboratory reference literacy

Ask what clinical question an investigation answers and whether the result changes treatment. For typical bronchiolitis, the clinical guidance describes no routine laboratory tests or radiological studies; chest imaging may be chosen in atypical or severe illness. Respiratory difficulty, pauses, exhaustion, poor feeding or dehydration need clinical assessment and may require hospital care. Do not let a reassuring home oximeter number erase visibly abnormal breathing or apnea, and do not supply a home oxygen-titration plan. Professional decisions about hydration or breathing support are not caregiver procedure instructions. The bronchiolitis pathway is distinct from a different child's established asthma rescue plan. S557 S552

A lab flag is not a diagnosis. Ask for analyte, result and units, specimen/date, the reporting laboratory's pediatric reference interval, exact age and relevant gestation/illness/treatment. Specimen and assay/instrument can matter; verify them when deciding whether a comparison is valid. Do not paste an adult range onto a neonatal/child result, infer danger from an isolated flag or order every possible confirmatory test. The SickKids CALIPER project provides pediatric reference intervals; its institutional explanation explicitly distinguishes child age/sex and instrument-specific comparison. The current service page confirms its pediatric reference role, but the 2018 launch article's database size and access/app details are historical and must not be claimed current. No database entries were copied or individually queried here. S570 S571

Similarly, heart/respiratory rates and other vital observations need age, resting/crying state, measurement method and clinical context. A caregiver count may be useful to report but should not become an autonomous score or delay care for obvious breathing difficulty. Normal-looking readings do not erase lethargy, apnea or refusal to feed. Ask why the clinician may repeat a measurement and what the actual follow-up plan is, instead of inventing a universal under-five normal-values table. Neonatal bilirubin illustrates why age at measurement and risk context belong with the result, rather than treating an adult reference flag as clearance. S565 S632

## Fever: treat discomfort, assess the whole child

Record temperature, device/site, time and duration. Do not confuse a reported warm forehead with a measured reading or recalculate one thermometer site into another with an invented correction. Offer usual fluids, check urine and ordinary interaction, and check the child during the night. A stable child who drinks and interacts normally can often be supported at home while improving. Hard-to-wake drowsiness, altered responsiveness, breathing difficulty, abnormal colour, stiff neck or a non-fading rash requires emergency escalation. Do not wait for a higher temperature or a complete rash to appear. Lowering a temperature does not establish that the illness is harmless. S548

Preserve the distinct young-infant findings above. For an older child, do not treat the temperature or its response to medicine as clearance when breathing, responsiveness or hydration is concerning. Offer suitable fever medicine for distress rather than to normalize a number. Do not recreate a clinical scoring table from missing observations or present a chat score as validated. Practical caregiver advice should avoid simultaneous paracetamol/ibuprofen and defer any alternating schedule to a verified health-professional plan. Do not promise that fever medicine prevents seizures. S548 S554

Fever lasting five days or more needs prompt clinical review, including consideration of Kawasaki disease. Ask about red eyes, cracked lips or mouth changes, rash, hand/foot changes and neck swelling, including findings that were present earlier and have faded. This combination is a reason for assessment, not a chat diagnosis. Kawasaki disease has no single diagnostic test; clinicians may select blood/urine tests and cardiac assessment. Hospital treatment can include intravenous medicine, aspirin and, for selected circumstances, steroids. Aspirin here is a supervised exception: do not start it at home because symptoms sound compatible, and do not automatically stop a child's prescribed specialist aspirin under a general childhood prohibition. S549

## Noisy breathing, croup and preschool wheeze

Ask whether the noise occurs on breathing in or out, at rest or only crying, and whether onset followed choking, allergen exposure or a cold. Grunting, marked pulling beneath/between ribs, pauses, inability to drink because of breathing, exhaustion, blue/grey colour or poor responsiveness needs emergency action. A quietening child with worsening effort is not necessarily recovering. Do not require a caregiver to count breaths or obtain a home saturation before escalating clear distress. A video may help a clinician later but must not delay care. S552 S550

A barking cough, hoarseness and inspiratory rasp can occur with croup, but other airway problems can resemble it. Suspected croup warrants timely professional advice because treatment may shorten or control illness. Keep the child calm, with the caregiver and in a comfortable upright position; offer fluid only when swallowing safely. Do not use steam inhalation. Drooling with difficulty swallowing, abnormal stillness, difficulty waking or substantial distress requires emergency assessment. A home label of croup must not clear sudden choking or anaphylaxis. S550

A clinician may give medicine to shorten or control croup, and a more seriously ill child may need hospital care. Ask what treatment was prescribed and the signs that require a return, rather than selecting leftover steroids or a home nebulized-adrenaline regimen. A diagnosis or discharge made earlier does not settle a new episode of drooling, poor responsiveness or substantial breathing difficulty. Detailed drug selection, observation intervals and investigation protocols are outside this packet. S550

Bronchiolitis mainly affects children under two, with particular vulnerability in young and premature infants; it also remains relevant to ages 12-23 months. Feeding/drinking less, reduced urine or worsening after a cold needs urgent review; do not wait for a fixed dry-nappy interval when other dehydration signs are already present. Symptoms often worsen around days three to five before improving. Support hydration and observation when a clinician considers home care suitable; higher-risk children may need hospital care. For young-infant apnea, call emergency services even without the expected cold/cough story. A four-year-old with wheeze should not automatically receive an infant bronchiolitis explanation. S552

Recurrent preschool wheeze needs review of pattern, triggers, feeding/function and treatment response, without claiming every wheezing toddler has asthma. Age matters: an infant bronchiolitis pathway must not automatically be applied to recurrent wheezing in an older child. Chat cannot auscultate crackles or settle the diagnosis remotely. Ask about a documented diagnosis and actual action plan, and arrange clinical review rather than inventing a new inhaler regimen. S297 S552

A child's existing prescribed reliever and spacer/action plan have a distinct pathway: help the caregiver follow that actual plan while arranging emergency help for severe or worsening breathing or poor response. Verify the inhaler and device instead of assuming colour identifies the ingredient or plan. Do not import a general adult AIR/MART puff schedule into a preschool child, borrow another child's inhaler, prescribe oral steroids or delay an established rescue action because of a general prescribing boundary. Recovery after an attack still warrants prompt review. S297

## Seizure with fever

During a seizure, call emergency services for a first event, breathing difficulty, a prolonged event approaching/exceeding five minutes, one-sided movements or repeated seizures without recovery. Time it, move hazards away, protect the head and stay with the child. Do not restrain, put a spoon/fingers into the mouth or give oral fluid/medicine. After jerking stops, if breathing normally, use an appropriate recovery position and monitor. If breathing is abnormal, follow the dispatcher/EMERGENCY pathway. Do not drive an actively seizing or unconscious child to hospital. S554

A first fever-associated seizure needs hospital assessment even if brief and followed by recovery. Recurrent seizures within 24 hours or unusual sleepiness persisting beyond the expected recovery require urgent escalation. Fever-associated seizures occur in this age band but cannot be diagnosed from a description; possible infection and other causes need consideration. Most do not require ongoing seizure treatment. A clinician may select tests from the examination rather than ordering blood, lumbar puncture and imaging for every child. If an individual rescue medicine has already been prescribed, support its verified instructions and emergency plan; do not invent a new benzodiazepine prescription or use another child's medicine. Antipyretics are not reliable seizure prevention. S554 S548

## Vomiting, diarrhea and hydration

Ask about frequency/trend, fluid retained, last urine, ordinary alertness, blood, pain and whether vomit is green or yellow-green. Green/bilious vomit, blood/coffee-ground vomit, sudden severe abdominal pain or altered responsiveness needs emergency assessment. Do not reassure that green vomit is a routine stomach bug, ask for a photograph before acting or offer a home obstruction test. Persistent inability to keep fluid down, blood in stool or dehydration in an under-five requires urgent professional advice. Usual diarrhea/vomiting duration is descriptive, not a permission to wait through deterioration. S462

For a child alert enough to swallow, offer regular small amounts of an age-appropriate oral rehydration solution selected with a pharmacist/clinician, prepared using the exact packet and water volume. Do not concentrate it, dilute formula, substitute sports drinks as equivalent ORS or give an improvised weight-based deficit calculation. Continue usual feeding as tolerated; a recovering child can resume usual food. Reduced urine, dry mouth, sunken eyes, few tears or unusual drowsiness are useful observations but cannot quantify dehydration from chat. Shock-like colour changes, cold skin, difficult breathing or difficulty waking need emergency help. ORS should not postpone assessment of a child who is unable to retain it or is deteriorating. S555

Avoid fruit juice/fizzy drinks during diarrhea and do not give toddler antidiarrheal medicines such as loperamide. Do not select antiemetics or antibiotics remotely; these are not automatic treatments for a presumed stomach virus. Explain that clinicians assess hydration and alternative causes, with further treatment chosen according to severity and findings. Wash hands with soap and water, clean contaminated surfaces and follow local childcare exclusion advice; the NHS describes at least 48 hours after the final diarrhea/vomiting episode, which must not be presented as every country's rule. S462

## Ear pain and clinician-selected antibiotics

Ear tugging or irritability alone cannot establish acute middle-ear infection. Examination for middle-ear fluid and appropriate eardrum findings matters. Distinguish observation after professional assessment from deciding at home that antibiotics are unnecessary. Explain that antibiotics can help appropriate bacterial presentations but also cause adverse effects and contribute to resistance; a cold does not itself need an antibiotic. US CDC guidance names amoxicillin as a common first option for eligible cases, with different choices when recent amoxicillin, purulent conjunctivitis, allergy or prior response changes the decision. These are clinician options, not a personalized prescription. S557

CDC's caregiver sheet supports clinician-agreed watchful waiting for selected mild illness: ages 6-23 months with one affected ear, or age two years and older with one or both, symptoms for less than two days, mild pain and temperature below 39 C. Thus a 20-month-old with both ears affected does not fit that example. Arrange reliable follow-up; the sheet describes two to three days of observation and earlier contact for high fever or ear drainage. Worsening or failure to improve requires review, rather than repeatedly extending the waiting period. The exact birthday boundary is clearer in the caregiver sheet than the clinical page's shorthand 'greater than two'. Do not translate these US eligibility examples into a universal antibiotic law. S556

## Urinary symptoms, pain and refusal to walk

Possible UTI in a toddler needs prompt clinical assessment. Ask about fever, painful/frequent urination, new accidents after established continence, vomiting, intake and abdominal/back pain. Young children may simply seem unwell or drink poorly. Smell alone is not a diagnosis. Fever with marked systemic illness follows the emergency pathway; inability to urinate with pain also needs immediate assessment. Do not suggest waiting for an adult pharmacy UTI pathway, cranberry treatment or leftover antibiotics. S471

A clinician may request urine testing when a child has suspected urinary infection. Ask what sample the service wants and how it should be collected; do not teach invasive sampling or diagnose from a strip photograph. Explain that the assessment and local pathway determine further tests and treatment. Do not offer every child a kidney-scan package, a full dipstick self-prescribing algorithm or an adult pharmacy pathway. A child who becomes drowsy, breathes abnormally or deteriorates needs urgent care without waiting to complete a home sample. S471 S565

A new unexplained limp needs examination. Inability to bear weight, severe sudden hip/knee/thigh pain, deformity, fever with illness or worsening warrants emergency assessment. A hot/swollen joint with fever and refusal to use the limb can represent serious infection; do not label it growing pains or transient synovitis from chat. Hip problems can be felt at the knee. Do not force walking, manipulate the joint or ask the caregiver to demonstrate provocative tests. Clinicians may select X-ray or specialist assessment from history/examination. S559 S548

Sudden severe testicular/scrotal pain, especially with nausea/vomiting or abdominal pain, requires emergency assessment because torsion is time-sensitive. A preschool child may describe the location imperfectly, so a new painful groin/scrotal complaint should not be dismissed. Do not request an intimate image, manipulate a testicle, recommend waiting for a routine ultrasound or reassure from visible appearance. Swelling or ongoing discomfort also needs prompt assessment. ## Medicines: identity before any instructions

Ask for every ingredient, exact concentration, form/route, market label, age, weight with units, amounts/times already administered and other products. A brand name or bottle volume is insufficient. Liquid mL and ingredient mg are different; do not calculate a pediatric dose from chat or scale down an adult dose. Follow the current product's age/weight instructions and pharmacist/clinician advice, using its supplied dosing device. A nonurgent label photo can help identify ingredients; do not require a child photo. Keep an administration record so caregivers do not accidentally repeat a dose. Unknown amount or possible overdose routes to poison advice now, even before symptoms.

For fever discomfort use only a suitable verified product. Do not routinely give paracetamol and ibuprofen simultaneously or create an alternating timetable. NHS cautions against ibuprofen with dehydration or chickenpox and recommends a doctor's advice in children with asthma. Aspirin is not routine fever/pain treatment in this band; specialist-prescribed exceptions require their own plan. Check contraindications and local labels rather than declaring a medicine safe just because age exceeds its minimum. S548 S549

US FDA advises against OTC cough/cold medicines below age two and describes manufacturer labels commonly saying not below four. Turning four does not guarantee suitability or benefit; check the exact product and professional advice. Combination products can duplicate acetaminophen/paracetamol or other ingredients. Use the dosing device supplied and do not use adult formulations. UK MHRA's ingredient-specific cough/cold guidance excludes the listed remedies below six; it is historical regulator guidance still hosted, not proof every listed ingredient remains marketed. Preserve country differences instead of selecting the loosest cutoff for an imported bottle. S560 S561

## Ingestion, burns, head injury and water safety

Suspected button battery ingestion needs immediate emergency-department/poison-center routing even if the child is playing normally or it looked like a coin. Do not induce vomiting or wait for stool passage. NCPC's specific protocol allows honey only when a lithium coin cell may have been swallowed, the child is at least 12 months, ingestion was within the previous 12 hours, swallowing is safe and honey is immediately available: 10 mL every ten minutes, up to six doses while going to emergency care. This verified lay protocol is distinct from pediatric prescription calculation. Honey slows injury; it does not replace removal or justify delay. Unknown time, unsafe swallowing or a younger child excludes this advice. Otherwise avoid oral intake pending professional directions. S562

Suspected one or more magnets swallowed also needs immediate medical assessment without waiting for symptoms. Multiple magnets may attract across intestinal walls and cause injury; an apparent single magnet is not permission for home waiting. Keep loose magnets and battery devices inaccessible, follow toy age warnings and check damaged toys. For other possible poisons/medicine errors, contact the local poison service with product, approximate amount, time, age/weight and symptoms; collapse, seizure, abnormal breathing or inability to wake means emergency services now. Do not induce vomiting or give a universal neutralizing drink. For eye exposure begin immediate water irrigation and obtain poison advice; do not defer a verified first-aid action until a full interview. S563 S278

Cool a thermal burn with cool running water for twenty minutes as soon as possible; remove nearby items unless stuck and cover loosely after cooling. Large/deep burns, face/genital involvement, chemicals or electricity need emergency assessment. Keep the rest of a young child's body warm while cooling the affected area. Use existing EMERGENCY guidance for additional first-aid boundaries; do not declare burn depth from a photo or replace cooling with cream. Under-five burn uncertainty warrants professional advice. S283

After head injury, loss of responsiveness, inability to stay awake, seizure, new weakness/walking difficulty or concerning mechanism needs emergency help. Vomiting or other concerning symptoms needs urgent advice even after an initially normal period. Do not use a pupil check, a phone conversation or a smiling photo as clearance. After professional minor-injury assessment follow the discharge plan and adult supervision; ordinary sleep is different from inability to wake normally. Ask what changed since discharge and escalate new warning signs. S285

For drowning prevention designate an attentive adult around all water, including baths; lessons and lifeguards do not replace close constant supervision. Use pool barriers and self-closing gates, suitable life jackets around natural water, and secure access after swimming. Inflatable toys are not safety devices. During an actual submersion emergency use safe rescue and the EMERGENCY module; do not enter dangerous water untrained, squeeze water out or delay breathing support. S564

## Implementation and evidence limits

This is an original navigation synthesis. It does not establish diagnoses, grant medical clearance, reproduce a proprietary assessment instrument or validate AI triage. Routes and synthetic fixtures test response behavior and age/localization boundaries; they are not clinical validation. Sources are selected official guidance and institutional pages, not a systematic review. Restricted-license clinical sources were removed after rights review. Exact local service access, product approvals and individual prescriptions need fresh verification. Where an independently supported precise protocol was unavailable, the packet retains safe navigation and removes that precision.

Photos are optional only for non-intimate, nonurgent label or visible-change descriptions. Never request genital images or delay emergency assessment for media. Actual child records remain private and require separate storage authorization. Reassess every new turn: a prior mild description cannot neutralize later drowsiness, green vomiting, breathing difficulty or ingestion concern. The assistant should explain uncertainty without burying a concrete action in an exhaustive differential.
