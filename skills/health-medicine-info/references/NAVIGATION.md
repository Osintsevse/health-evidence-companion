# Finding the right health service while at home or travelling

Reviewed 2026-10-09. This module supports service navigation, not diagnosis, facility endorsement, booking, or a guarantee of access, quality, coverage, price, language, or availability. It separates emergency dispatch from non-emergency facility search. Use the person's **current physical location** and immediate needs; profile residence, nationality, account location, preferred language, and a map default may all differ. S489 S289

## Safety comes before searching

For suspected stroke, heart attack, severe breathing difficulty, collapse, major injury, dangerous bleeding, severe allergic reaction, acute pregnancy emergency, or another rapidly worsening/life-threatening state, call the local emergency number now. Give the exact current location first and follow the dispatcher. The dispatcher and local emergency system choose ambulance response, transport, and destination based on the patient's condition and local capacity. Do not send the person to compare hospitals, self-drive if unstable, wait for insurer approval, search for a preferred language, gather photographs, or complete paperwork before calling. Another person can contact an insurer or locate documents in parallel if doing so does not distract from care. WHO describes emergency care as a connected pathway from scene and transport through emergency unit and early inpatient care, rather than a directory choice alone. S489 S490

If symptoms are concerning but not clearly an immediate emergency, do not declare them safe from chat. Ask what is happening, when it began, whether it is worsening, age, pregnancy where relevant, and whether the person can safely travel. Recommend local clinical advice promptly when uncertainty remains; make red-flag changes explicit. If new danger signs appear, switch to the emergency pathway. Avoid making a facility search, translation, map result, insurance call, or estimated distance a prerequisite for urgent assessment. A phone service may triage and direct to a locally suitable service; examples such as NHS 111 are specific to their own jurisdiction, not a universal telephone number. S494 S492

An assistant must not imply it called, confirmed, reserved, or arranged anything unless an authorized tool actually completed that action and its result was read back. In this packet, the assistant only explains how the person or their chosen helper can verify details. For a caller with limited local language, give a short script in their language to read or show, but do not claim that emergency dispatch or a facility will provide an interpreter unless verified.

## Choosing a type of care

Names such as "urgent care," "walk-in," "clinic," "emergency room," "hospital," and "medical centre" are not interchangeable across countries. Treat a label as a search hint, not proof of capability. Local systems decide scope. The NHS, for example, distinguishes life-threatening emergencies routed to ambulance/A&E from urgent non-life-threatening care that may use 111, a GP, pharmacy, or an urgent treatment centre. Those definitions illustrate one system only. S492 S493 S494

- **Emergency department / emergency unit:** intended for acute assessment and stabilisation, with scope, specialist cover, diagnostics and onward referral depending on the facility, time, and system. A building called a hospital does not prove that its emergency unit is open or can deliver a particular specialty or procedure.
- **Urgent treatment / immediate-care centre:** may assess selected same-day, non-life-threatening problems. Its permitted ages, tests, hours, referral rules, and ability to manage deterioration vary. Confirm the exact service and eligibility before a non-emergency visit.
- **Primary care / GP / family doctor:** often handles routine, preventive, and continuing care, and may triage urgent problems during opening hours. Access rules and same-day capacity vary. It is not a substitute for an ambulance when symptoms are life-threatening.
- **Specialist or diagnostic clinic:** appropriate only when the needed specialty, age group, test, referral route, and timing are confirmed. A search result that mentions neurology, cardiology, obstetrics, or imaging does not show that emergency-capable service is available now.
- **Pharmacy / advice line / telehealth:** useful for some lower-acuity questions when local rules and symptoms fit. They cannot replace in-person emergency assessment for severe or rapidly worsening illness.

For suspected stroke or acute coronary syndrome, do not independently select a centre on the basis of a marketing claim such as "stroke hospital" or "cardiac centre." Emergency dispatch may route to an appropriate facility, including a centre with a required capability, based on local protocols and availability. If the person has already been assessed or discharged, follow the clinician's referral and return precautions; ask the referring service how to reach the recommended receiving unit. Availability of a stroke team, catheterisation lab, paediatric service, obstetric emergency, blood bank, ICU, imaging modality, or specialist overnight is time-dependent and must be verified. This module does not maintain a quality ranking or live capability registry. S490 S489

## A reproducible search for non-emergency care

Use a search only after deciding that it will not delay emergency action. Ask only for enough context to make the search relevant. A useful first exchange is: "Where are you physically now (country and city/area, or a nearby landmark)? Is it okay to use that location for this search? What care is needed, how soon, and for what age group?" Do not assume that a home address, GPS setting, language, or nationality identifies the current location. A precise location is useful for a short-lived local result; ask consent before using or repeating it, and do not retain it in public material.

Then establish, as relevant: (1) urgency and trajectory; (2) adult/child and pregnancy/postpartum status; (3) the exact service or specialty requested; (4) capabilities that matter, such as X-ray, laboratory tests, ultrasound, emergency department, paediatric assessment, maternity care, dialysis, or a specific already-recommended service; (5) mobility, wheelchair access, stairs, communication/hearing access, and companion needs; (6) preferred language and whether an interpreter is needed; (7) opening now versus appointment availability; and (8) safe transport and ability to travel. Do not collect a full medical history, passport, insurance policy, or exact apartment number when it does not change the next step. Do not ask the user to share sensitive records or images merely to locate a clinic.

Search in a transparent sequence:

1. **Find the official health-system route.** Search the country's or region's health ministry, regulator, public health service, municipal health authority, or recognized provider directory. Search in a language the person can use, and in the local official language where useful. For example, Thailand's Ministry of Public Health GIS Health service allows searches for facility types and names; the NHS publishes urgent/emergency service guidance and England's Directory of Services supports its own 111 pathway. These are examples of local sources, not global directories. S496 S491 S495
2. **Use maps and general web search to discover candidates.** Google Maps is broadly useful worldwide; 2GIS or Yandex Maps may add local coverage in Russia and nearby markets. Thailand searches can include Thai-language terms or copied local names to improve discovery, but this is optional. Do not make Thai fluency or a machine-translated diagnosis a condition of getting help. Search combinations can include a plain-language symptom/service and the city/area, e.g., "urgent clinic," "emergency department," "pediatric clinic," "pharmacy," or the local equivalent. Do not infer suitability from a sponsored result, star rating, review, tourist-English label, or nearest pin. Map and organization data may be user-edited, owner-supplied, incomplete, or stale. Google documents that place names, addresses and hours can be edited; Yandex describes contact and opening-hour information in organization cards. Thus maps are discovery aids, not official confirmation. S502 S503
3. **Confirm the exact branch from its own current source.** Open the facility's own branch page or the responsible public directory entry. Match full name, branch, street address, and phone; beware of similarly named hospital chains and map pins at an office rather than the emergency entrance. If it is not an emergency and a phone call is practical, ask whether the needed service is operating now, accepts this age group and walk-ins, requires referral/appointment/payment, has the relevant clinician/test, can communicate in the preferred language or arrange an interpreter, and has an accessible entrance. Ask whether the listed address is the correct arrival point. Do not treat an unanswered phone as proof of closure or readiness; use a verified alternate official contact or local advice service.
4. **Check travel and access separately.** A map's distance is a route estimate from the selected origin and mode, not an ambulance response time. Confirm whether the patient can safely use that mode; public transport or a taxi is not a substitute for emergency medical transport if unstable. Check accessibility and exact entry/registration desk. For nonurgent care, compare realistic time and service fit, not distance alone.
5. **Present uncertainty with the result.** State what source directly confirms each fact and when it was checked. If a fact appears only in a map listing or search snippet, label it "map listing, unconfirmed." If current official details conflict, show the conflict and advise use of the local health line, regulator, or direct verified branch contact. Do not silently choose the more convenient answer.

If the local language is unfamiliar, the assistant can draft a short bilingual phrase with plain descriptions rather than a speculative diagnosis: "I am at [landmark/address]. The person is [age]-year-old. They have [plain symptom] since [time]. It is [getting worse / stable]. Where should we go now? Is this service open, and can you receive [age group]?" For an emergency, tell the user to call first and read the location and symptoms; do not keep them in chat polishing the translation. For a person in Thailand with an acute medical emergency, lead with the Thai government's listed EMS number 1669; tell them to give their exact location and follow dispatch. Do not ask a distressed caller to arbitrate web sources or search for a hospital. Evidence note: the Thai government source lists 1669; current GOV.UK travel advice also says to dial 191 and ask for an ambulance while listing 1669. This discrepancy is recorded for pre-travel awareness and is not a reason to delay. Language support is unconfirmed. S497 S498 S499

## Emergency numbers: give bounded examples, not a worldwide list

The current country determines the number. If location is unknown, say "Call your local emergency number now" and ask for country/city in parallel. If a trusted companion is nearby, they can check the number while the user starts the call. Do not tell anyone to call a number just to test it. Clearly label every numeric example by jurisdiction and date/source:

- **EU:** 112 is the free emergency number available throughout EU countries. Operators in many countries may answer in another language, but language ability is not guaranteed. This does not mean that 112 is a universal number worldwide. S289
- **Thailand:** For an acute medical emergency, lead with the Thai government-listed EMS number 1669 and tell the caller to give the exact location and follow dispatch. Do not make a stressed caller compare sources. Evidence note: current GOV.UK travel advice also says to dial 191 and ask for an ambulance while listing 1669; Thai government information lists 1669. English-language response is not guaranteed. S497 S498 S499
- **Serbia:** the Serbian government's current public information lists 194 for ambulance and 112 for emergency interventions; identify which number you recommend only after confirming current local instructions. The government source describes free calls but is not a claim about response time or service availability. S290
- **Russia:** the Russian Ministry of Emergency Situations states 112 for emergency services and 103 or other regional numbers for ambulance. Check current regional guidance and mobile access; do not infer language support. S500

These examples are not a static global directory. Emergency-number information can change, differ by service and network, and be unavailable through some devices or locations. For other countries, use the country's current official government/health-service instructions; if immediate danger exists, use the emergency number shown by local authorities or ask a nearby trusted person to call emergency dispatch. Do not rely on a browser snippet alone. EU 112 rules and each country's emergency-service organisation are distinct. S289 S500

## Travel insurance and payment

Once emergency care is underway - or for an appropriate non-emergency visit - check the policy's emergency-assistance number from the insurer's own card/app/site or policy document. Ask the insurer or assistance company about the nearest suitable network facility, direct billing or guarantee-of-payment process, pre-authorisation, deductible/co-payment, exclusions, pre-existing conditions, pregnancy/sports exclusions, evacuation/repatriation, receipts and claim deadlines. Record the name/ID of the representative, time, reference number, and instructions if the patient or companion can do so without delaying care. Policy wording and local facility practice control; a call does not guarantee payment, direct billing, a particular hospital, or approval. Do not tell someone to choose a medically unsuitable provider solely because it is in-network, or to postpone medically needed emergency assessment while seeking approval. The Thailand travel guidance specifically advises contacting the insurance or assistance company quickly if referred for treatment; that is an administrative step alongside care, not a reason to wait to call emergency dispatch. S499 S501

The UK EHIC/GHIC example is deliberately narrow: it can give eligible holders access to state-provided care in countries where the card applies, on local terms, but it is not a substitute for travel insurance, does not cover every cost, and does not cover repatriation. It is not worldwide free treatment and does not imply entitlement for every traveller or private facility. Check eligibility, destination, service type, residence status, and current rules with the responsible authority. Never promise reimbursement based solely on possession of a card. S501

## How to report candidate facilities

For requested real-world research, present a compact table with one row per candidate and this minimum evidence:

| Facility / exact branch | Address and distance | Service fit | Hours / phone | Official source checked | Checked at | Uncertainty / next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exact facility name and branch, or "unconfirmed candidate" | Full address; distance only if the origin was supplied/consented and route source/mode are stated | What the official source says; do not infer unlisted capabilities | Quote current official listing; say "not confirmed" when unavailable | Direct branch page, government/regulator directory, or health service | Date/time and whether page was opened or merely indexed | E.g. call to confirm service, age, walk-in rule, language, entrance, insurance terms |

For an emergency request, do not fill this table before giving immediate steps. For non-emergency requests, a short list of one to three well-supported options is usually easier to act on than a directory dump. Include alternatives if a source is stale, conflicting, or not accessible. Do not rank hospitals by online reviews, tourist-facing marketing, building size, map distance, or a claimed specialty badge. A quality claim requires credible, relevant, comparable evidence; if that evidence is absent, say so. Do not fabricate facility names, addresses, phone numbers, opening times, prices, accepted insurance, service capacity, appointment availability, language support, or map distances.

## Boundaries and fallback

A request for "the best hospital" needs a defined clinical/service need, urgency, location, and decision constraint. Ask what "best" means (e.g., emergency dispatch destination, paediatric service, language, accessible site, public coverage) and explain when the evidence cannot support a comparative quality ranking. A user can choose a non-emergency facility; an emergency dispatcher makes real-time transport decisions. Do not override an active clinician's referral with a map search.

If official sources are missing or contradictory, state exactly what was verified and what was not. For example: "The ministry directory lists this branch, but I could not confirm that its imaging unit is open tonight. Use the local health advice line or call the facility's verified switchboard before travelling if symptoms remain stable. If symptoms become severe or worsen rapidly, call emergency services." If web access is unavailable, give a safe search plan and explain that live details remain unverified. Do not turn uncertainty into a reassuring claim.

Do not infer that a facility is safe or high quality from licensing alone; licensing verifies a regulatory status, not an individual's current clinical fit or performance. Do not suggest that a map route guarantees accessibility, transport, or prompt care. Do not ask the user to disclose a full address to the assistant when a nearby intersection/landmark is enough; for emergency dispatch, the caller should give dispatch the precise location. Never place calls, send personal data, share policy documents, or book care without explicit authorization and an actually available authorized channel. Maintain a clear distinction between a lookup, an attempted contact, a confirmed answer, and a completed appointment.

## Evidence and scope

WHO's framework supports thinking of emergency care across scene, transport, facility emergency care and early inpatient care. Local pathways differ; the examples in this module illustrate how to verify a route, not a global standard of facility naming or capability. NHS examples show how one national system routes emergencies and urgent needs through distinct services and a directory linked to triage. Thai, Serbian, Russian and EU contact examples are jurisdiction-bound and should be rechecked before use. The Thai government number page inspected for this packet states an update date of 2023-07-24. A current Thai Ministry of Public Health hospital contact page, updated 2026-09-26, also lists 1669, but is provider-level corroboration rather than a national EMS policy page. The GOV.UK page provides separate travel advice, not Thai primary guidance. The packet describes verification and communication practice; it does not prescribe treatment, decide diagnosis, triage a particular patient, or guarantee clinical outcomes. S490 S495 S496 S290 S500
