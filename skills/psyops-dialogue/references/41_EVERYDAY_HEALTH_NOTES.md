## Adult heartburn and reflux

Burning behind the breastbone and regurgitation can occur with reflux; they do not prove its cause. Difficulty/pain swallowing, persistent vomiting, unexplained weight loss, appetite loss and gastrointestinal bleeding warrant assessment; persistent symptoms despite self-care also merit review. Blood/coffee-ground vomit or black tarry stool belongs in the urgent bleeding pathway. These selected NIDDK adult symptom sections do not cover infants. [medical:S645]

Use the chest-emergency pathway for new concerning pressure-like chest symptoms, spreading pain, sweating or breathlessness. A heart attack can resemble indigestion; do not reassure from the word 'heartburn', prior reflux or symptom improvement. See the existing EMERGENCY chest section. [medical:S276]

NIDDK describes lifestyle options and antacid, H2-blocker and proton-pump-inhibitor classes. This does not select a product, dose or duration. It advises clinical discussion for daily antacid use, severe symptoms or symptoms persisting despite nonprescription products. Surgical/procedure evidence was not appraised here. [medical:S646]

For nighttime/lying-down symptoms, NIDDK says leaving at least three hours between a meal and lying down may help. Identify plausible personal food/drink triggers and consider a focused change rather than a long universal elimination list. These pages were reviewed July 2020; today's retrieval does not make them a 2026 guideline. Refresh local recommendations and exact labels before treatment advice. [medical:S647]

## Sleep habits and chronic insomnia

General habits include adequate sleep opportunity, reasonably consistent timing, a quiet/dark/cool environment and a calm wind-down. Daytime outdoor time/activity may support a routine. Consider caffeine/nicotine timing, alcohol and large late meals. If naps interfere with nighttime sleep, review timing/length; shift work needs a schedule-specific approach. These habits are not rigid rules or a guarantee of good sleep. [medical:S648]

NHLBI describes multicomponent CBT-I as a usual first treatment for long-term insomnia. Sleep education/habits are one component alongside other structured elements. Persistent difficulty with daytime effects deserves assessment rather than repeated hygiene tips. Do not convert sleep restriction into an unsupervised time-in-bed prescription here, independently select sleep medicines or extrapolate this adult overview to infants. The page was updated March 2022; a full current insomnia guideline, sleep-apnea pathway and comparative trial review remain pending. [medical:S649]

## Eye exercises: a named indication

NEI describes convergence insufficiency as a problem coordinating the eyes for near work. Blur/double vision, tired eyes or close-work difficulty can prompt assessment, but do not establish the diagnosis. Assessment includes specific convergence/alignment and focusing measurements; normal sharpness on a standard vision test does not settle it. [medical:S650]

For diagnosed convergence insufficiency, exercises may involve specialist sessions and home practice; other options depend on assessment. This supports a condition-specific explanation, not a claim that generic exercises reverse myopia, astigmatism or presbyopia or improve everyone's eyesight. The overview was read; linked trials/comparative efficacy were not independently appraised. Sudden vision loss, painful red eye or injury still follows existing urgent eye routing. [medical:S650]

## Dandruff and scalp symptoms

The federal MedlinePlus summary describes white/yellow flaking, sometimes itchy, commonly associated with seborrheic dermatitis. It distinguishes infant cradle cap and lists scalp ringworm and psoriasis among alternatives. Flaking or a photo cannot settle the diagnosis. Contact a clinician when dandruff shampoo does not control symptoms; persistent or atypical symptoms should not be treated as established dandruff. [medical:S651]

This note does not establish a preferred shampoo ingredient, frequency, contact time or infant regimen. Those need age/product-specific evidence and labels. Only the federal summary was used; linked A.D.A.M., AAD and other third-party treatment pages are not incorporated. Source: MedlinePlus, National Library of Medicine. [medical:S651]

## Tick bites: removal, follow-up and geography

Inspect the body after exposure and remove attached ticks promptly. CDC describes grasping close to the skin with tweezers and pulling steadily without twisting/crushing; clean the site and hands. Do not use heat, petroleum jelly or nail polish to force detachment. Rash or fever in the following days/weeks merits assessment, reporting exposure date/location. CDC cautions against treatment decisions based on commercial tick tests: an infected tick does not prove human infection, and a negative result can falsely reassure. Its pathway is US-specific. [medical:S652]

ECDC supports protective clothing, suitable repellents, inspection and prompt straight removal in European prevention advice. Product suitability/instructions matter. These pages do not establish worldwide antibiotic, testing or vaccination rules. Check country, timing and current local disease guidance; Serbian/Russian clinical protocols were not verified here. [medical:S653; medical:S652]

## Snake bites: immediate care and regional limits

Suspected venomous snakebite needs emergency care. Move away from the snake, keep the person still, remove tight items near the bite, arrange urgent transport through local emergency services and monitor breathing/responsiveness. Do not delay for species identification or handle/capture the snake. Follow dispatch and existing resuscitation guidance for abnormal breathing. [medical:S654; medical:S655; medical:S656]

WHO warns against cutting/suction, narrow tourniquets, chemicals and traditional remedies. Pressure pads/compression are not universal: WHO limits applicability by venom effects and warns about tissue-damaging venom. This generic note does not teach the technique. Venom/antivenom applicability varies by species and geography; local clinicians select treatment. No antivenom or oral-medicine regimen is supplied. The Q&A is dated November 2019; the topic page has no displayed review date. Current local protocols remain necessary. [medical:S655; medical:S654]
