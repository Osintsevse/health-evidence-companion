# Endometriosis: diagnosis, treatment choices, fertility and emerging evidence

Evidence checked: 9 October 2026. This is general education and a clinician-discussion resource. It is not a diagnostic instrument, prescription, medical clearance or substitute for clinical care. Treatment selection requires the person's anatomy, symptoms, pregnancy intentions, other conditions, medicines and local product information.

## What treatment can achieve

Endometriosis has effective treatment options. Saying that there is "no treatment" confuses the absence of a universal cure with the availability of care that can reduce pain, improve daily functioning, manage organ involvement and support fertility. Medical treatment, surgery, fertility treatment and multidisciplinary pain care address different objectives. None guarantees permanent elimination of every lesion or every contributor to pain. A treatment can be worthwhile even when it controls rather than eradicates a chronic condition. S361

A useful consultation separates four questions: What might explain the symptoms? Is an organ or pregnancy at immediate risk? What outcome matters now? Which approach offers an acceptable balance of benefit, burden and future options? These questions prevent a visible lesion from becoming the sole measure of success and prevent an unremarkable scan from ending an appropriate assessment.

Goals can include attending school, sleeping, having comfortable sex, reducing bleeding, maintaining bowel or bladder function, conceiving, avoiding pregnancy, or avoiding another operation. They may change over time. A person who wants pregnancy now needs a different discussion from someone prioritizing menstrual suppression for several years. Neither goal should be assumed from age, relationship status, gender identity or the presence of a uterus.

## Anatomy, biology and why symptoms vary

The endometrium is the lining within the uterine cavity. Endometriosis involves tissue resembling endometrium outside that location. Common phenotypes include superficial peritoneal lesions, ovarian endometriomas and deep disease involving structures such as the uterosacral ligaments, rectovaginal region, bowel, bladder or ureters. An endometrioma is an ovarian cyst associated with endometriosis; it is not the same as every functional or hemorrhagic ovarian cyst. Lesions, inflammation, fibrosis and adhesions can affect organs and their movement. S361 S365

The disease is biologically heterogeneous. Estrogen signaling, altered immune responses, tissue remodeling and local inflammatory processes are important, but a single proven cause does not explain all presentations. Retrograde menstruation is one proposed pathway, not a sufficient explanation for every case. Research examining approximately 370,000 cells identified distinct epithelial, stromal, immune and vascular environments in different lesion types. Such molecular findings help develop hypotheses; they do not establish that an individual has a particular immune defect or that an experimental target is already a treatment. S386

Pain can reflect more than the amount of visible disease. A prospective surgical cohort found an association between higher baseline central-sensitization questionnaire scores and more persistent pain after surgery. This observational association cannot establish causation or identify an individual who should be denied surgery. It supports asking about overlapping pain, sleep, muscle tenderness and functional limitations alongside lesion assessment. Persistent pain is not evidence that symptoms are imagined, and a questionnaire is not a substitute for examination or evaluation of new pathology. S387

Adenomyosis is a related but distinct condition involving endometrial glands and stroma within the uterine muscle. It can coexist with endometriosis and contribute to painful or heavy periods and uterine enlargement. Ultrasound and MRI can support assessment, although imaging criteria and performance vary. Removing extrauterine endometriosis does not automatically treat disease within the uterine wall. Treatment discussions should therefore distinguish the suspected source of symptoms and the implications of uterine surgery for future pregnancy. S367

## Frequency, recognition and symptom patterns

WHO estimates that endometriosis affects about 10% of reproductive-age women globally, approximately 190 million people. This is an estimate across a condition that is often undiagnosed; it is not a diagnostic probability for an individual with pelvic pain. WHO reports diagnostic delays of approximately 4–12 years, influenced by symptom normalization, limited awareness, access barriers and limitations of diagnostic pathways. Some people have little or no pain, and infertility investigations may first raise suspicion. S361

Menstrual association is useful but is not mandatory. Patterns worth discussing include severe dysmenorrhea, pain beginning before bleeding, chronic or intermittent pelvic pain, deep pain during or after penetration, painful bowel movements, urinary pain, fatigue and impaired daily function. Symptoms may persist between periods, during hormonal suppression or after surgery. A symptom diary can describe timing and impact; it cannot confirm endometriosis. The absence of a perfectly regular cycle should not exclude further assessment. S362 S364

For bowel complaints, distinguish pain with defecation, constipation or diarrhea, bloating, rectal bleeding and obstructive symptoms. For urinary complaints, distinguish burning, frequency, visible blood, flank pain, infection symptoms and retention. A person may have endometriosis together with irritable bowel syndrome, bladder pain, infection or pelvic-floor dysfunction. Menstrual timing does not prove the cause of bleeding or remove the need to investigate an alternative disease. New symptoms should be evaluated on their own merits.

Deep disease can affect an organ despite modest pain. Ureteral involvement can cause obstruction and hydronephrosis, sometimes silently, with risk to kidney function. Suspected urinary-tract involvement requires specialist assessment of the tract and kidneys; a symptom-only monitoring plan is inadequate when obstruction is possible. Bowel or urinary surgery requires a team with the relevant expertise and a discussion of organ-preserving options and complications. S366

Extrapelvic endometriosis can involve the diaphragm, thorax or scars. Recurrent menstrual-associated shoulder or chest symptoms, pneumothorax, coughing blood, or a painful swelling in a surgical scar deserve appropriate specialist evaluation. These patterns are clues rather than proof. Acute breathlessness or chest pain needs urgent assessment before attributing it to a cyclical condition. S363 S368

## Urgent alternatives and differential diagnosis

A prior diagnosis of endometriosis does not explain every later episode of pain. Seek emergency assessment for severe or rapidly worsening pelvic or abdominal pain, collapse or marked faintness, major bleeding, breathing difficulty, or severe pain with possible pregnancy. Sudden unilateral pelvic pain with vomiting may require urgent exclusion of torsion or a cyst complication. Pregnancy-related pain, shoulder-tip pain or fainting can signal ectopic pregnancy even when pregnancy was unexpected. Do not delay help to obtain a photograph, complete a diary or wait for the next period. S368

Fever, persistent vomiting, concerning discharge, inability to pass urine or stool, and visible blood in urine or stool warrant prompt clinical triage. The appropriate service depends on severity and local access. Important alternatives include pregnancy complications, pelvic infection, appendicitis, urinary infection or stones, gastrointestinal disease and ovarian pathology. Heavy bleeding also requires assessment of its cause and impact. A chat cannot reliably distinguish these conditions by pain location alone. S368

For nonurgent symptoms, the differential is broader than endometriosis versus "nothing": primary dysmenorrhea, adenomyosis, fibroids, ovarian cysts, bowel conditions, bladder disorders, pelvic-floor or musculoskeletal pain and neuropathic pain may overlap. Testing should answer a specific clinical question. Neither an endometriosis label nor a negative endometriosis investigation should prevent reassessment when the pattern changes.

## From suspicion to a useful diagnosis

A history should establish symptom onset, relation to bleeding, pain between periods, bowel and urinary symptoms, sexual pain when relevant, missed work or school, bleeding pattern and prior treatments. Ask whether earlier treatments were actually taken, tolerated and helpful, rather than labeling every unsuccessful prescription as biological treatment failure. Previous operations, pathology and imaging reports can prevent repetition and reveal what was or was not assessed.

Pregnancy possibility, contraception needs and reproductive goals belong in the assessment, with consent and privacy. Family history, adolescent symptoms, other pain conditions and medicine contraindications may change the pathway. A clinician may consider tests for pregnancy, infection, anemia or another suspected condition. These tests investigate alternatives or consequences; they do not form a universal endometriosis blood panel.

An abdominal and, when appropriate and consented to, pelvic examination may identify tenderness, a mass or reduced mobility, but normal findings do not exclude disease. Examination should be explained, adapted to age and anatomy, and stopped if consent is withdrawn. A person should not be required to undergo an intolerable internal examination before symptoms are taken seriously. Available imaging alternatives and their limitations can be discussed.

NICE recommends ultrasound in suspected endometriosis and cautions that a normal examination or ultrasound does not rule it out. Specialist transvaginal ultrasound or MRI can help assess deep disease, and investigation and referral can proceed alongside initial treatment. CA125 should not be used to diagnose endometriosis. Persistent symptoms, impaired daily function, treatment intolerance or suspected deep disease justify further assessment rather than repeated reassurance from the same negative scan. S362

Imaging answers different questions with different reliability. A specialist scan may detect an endometrioma or map rectal, bladder or other deep lesions well while missing superficial peritoneal disease. Performance depends on anatomical site, technique, reader expertise and the studied population. The 2024 multisociety consensus supports trained operators and structured mapping; accuracy reported in referral centers should not be treated as a universal property of every pelvic scan. MRI is complementary, not an automatic superior test for every suspected lesion. S365

A useful report should describe the question asked, structures assessed, findings and limitations. "No ovarian cyst" is not equivalent to "no endometriosis." Conversely, a nonspecific imaging finding is not proof that it causes all symptoms. Imaging classifications can support surgical planning, but classification alone does not determine pain severity, pregnancy prospects or whether surgery is necessary.

The 2026 ACOG announcement describes a shift toward presumptive clinical diagnosis using symptoms, history and examination, enabling empirical treatment while evaluation continues. This packet inspected the official announcement rather than the full new clinical practice guideline. It therefore does not attribute unverified detailed recommendations to that document. Clinical diagnosis is a legitimate working approach, with follow-up and reconsideration if the response or course is unexpected. S364

Laparoscopy remains an option when it could resolve uncertainty or offer treatment. It is not obligatory before every initial treatment, and it is not automatically the next step after any normal scan. Discussion should cover the probability of finding treatable disease, alternatives, surgical expertise, biopsy, organ involvement and the possibility of persistent pain. Suspected deep disease benefits from preoperative mapping. A well-documented normal systematic laparoscopy makes endometriosis less likely; negative histology from a particular sample does not invariably exclude it. S362 S363

## Choosing treatment without turning information into a prescription

Before comparing medicines, define the target and a review plan. Is the main problem menstrual pain, continuous pain, bleeding, contraception, organ involvement or infertility? Which side effects would be unacceptable? Are daily tablets, a device, injections or a procedure acceptable? What treatment and monitoring are affordable and accessible? A person can reasonably prioritize avoiding a particular adverse effect over the option with the most convenient schedule.

Nonhormonal analgesia can be part of an initial approach. NICE includes paracetamol or an NSAID as options, with review if inadequate. Selection needs ordinary medication safety checks, including gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney disease, anticoagulants, pregnancy possibility, allergy and duplicate ingredients. An analgesic response does not diagnose the cause of pain. This resource does not select a product, combine analgesics or provide an individualized dose. S362

Heat, manageable physical activity, sleep support and adaptations at school or work may help a person function while assessment proceeds. Persistent pain may justify assessment of pelvic-floor and musculoskeletal contributors and access to pain, physiotherapy or psychological support. These approaches should have explicit goals and be reviewed for benefit. They should not be presented as proof that pain is psychological, as a way to remove lesions, or as a condition that must be completed before appropriate gynecological care. S361 S387

Hormonal treatment usually aims to reduce cyclic stimulation or suppress ovarian hormonal activity. Combined hormonal contraception and progestogens are commonly discussed options for pain; continuous combined use may help menstrual symptoms. A levonorgestrel intrauterine system or etonogestrel implant may be suitable for some people. Choice depends on bleeding preferences, contraception, tolerability and contraindications. These options are not interchangeable with every formulation containing the same hormone, and use for endometriosis may be outside a particular local license. S363

Estrogen-containing options require particular attention to migraine with aura, venous or arterial thrombotic history and risk, hypertension, smoking at older reproductive ages, liver disease and hormone-sensitive malignancy. The CDC contraceptive eligibility framework distinguishes methods and conditions; it should not be flattened into "all hormones are unsafe" or "progestin is always safe." Current breast cancer, for example, is a major constraint on hormonal contraception. Contraceptive eligibility categories do not replace the separate label for a GnRH combination product. S369


Dienogest is one progestogen used for endometriosis in some jurisdictions. The inspected Croatian Visanne label illustrates an important distinction: the endometriosis product is not a contraceptive despite effects on ovulation. That document has a 2020 approval date and is not proof of current authorization or stock in another country. Exact product information must be checked locally, including bleeding, mood, bone and contraindication considerations. S370

GnRH agonists and antagonists suppress gonadotropin signaling through different mechanisms. Agonists can produce an initial stimulation before suppression; antagonists act through receptor blockade. They are commonly considered when other options are inadequate or unsuitable because hypoestrogenic effects, especially bone loss and vasomotor symptoms, matter. Add-back hormone treatment can reduce some of these burdens. It is a deliberately selected component of care, with its own contraindications, not an instruction to combine leftover hormones. S371 S373 S375

Aromatase inhibitors are a specialist option for pain refractory to other medical or surgical approaches, rather than routine first-line care. In premenopausal patients, ESHRE discusses them with another agent that suppresses ovarian stimulation. Evidence, adverse effects and off-label status require an explicit conversation. This is not a basis for repurposing an oncology medicine or copying a regimen from a research paper. Availability of a biologically plausible drug does not establish that its benefits outweigh harms for an individual. S363

Treatment response is assessed through symptoms and functioning as well as tolerability. Amenorrhea does not prove that every lesion is inactive, and continued pain does not automatically mean that a stronger suppressive medicine or more extensive operation will help. There is no reason to repeat an ineffective approach indefinitely without reconsidering the diagnosis, associated conditions and goals.

Contraception provides a concrete example of why country and label matter. US Myfembree requires effective nonhormonal contraception during treatment and for one week after stopping. These statements should not be merged into one universal instruction. Bone risk is not captured by a single average trial result. Prior fractures, low bone density, other medicines, nutrition, smoking and expected treatment duration may change the decision. New severe mood symptoms or suicidal thinking require prompt clinical help, not simply waiting for the next routine review. Drug-interaction checking must include prescription medicines, supplements and any hormonal contraception or add-back already being used. S372 S371

## What pivotal and comparative trials actually show

**Relugolix combination therapy, SPIRIT 1 and 2.** These placebo-controlled phase 3 trials enrolled 638 and 623 participants. At 24 weeks, dysmenorrhea response was approximately 75% with combination therapy versus 27% and 30% with placebo. Nonmenstrual pelvic-pain response was approximately 58% versus 40%, and 66% versus 43%. Response required pain improvement with stable or reduced analgesic use; it did not mean cure, absence of lesions or restored fertility. Bone-density change, adverse effects and withdrawal still matter. These were selected trial populations with confirmed disease, and the trials do not establish superiority to every first-line hormonal option. Funding was from Myovant. The abstract was inspected; a full independent reanalysis was not performed. S376

**Linzagolix, EDELWEISS 3.** In a 486-participant randomized trial, the higher-dose regimen with add-back produced dysmenorrhea response in 72.9% versus 23.5% with placebo and nonmenstrual pain response in 47.3% versus 30.9% at three months. The lower-dose monotherapy arm did not meet the nonmenstrual-pain coprimary endpoint at that time. These findings do not justify treating both trial regimens as equivalent licensed options. The study assessed symptom responses and safety over months, not permanent disease eradication, spontaneous conception or live birth. S377

**Linzagolix, EDELWEISS 6.** This 2026 extension followed selected completers to 12 months. Reported dysmenorrhea and nonmenstrual-pain responses for the higher-dose add-back group were 91% and 67.6%. There was no continuing placebo comparison, and entry into an extension selects people who complete and tolerate earlier treatment. The figures should not be presented as an individual's probability of success or compared directly with response rates from a different trial. The publication reports industry relationships and sponsorship; long-term comparative outcomes remain important. S378

**Postoperative contraception, PRE-EMPT.** This pragmatic randomized trial compared long-acting progestogens with a combined oral contraceptive after conservative surgery in 405 participants. At three years, pain outcomes were similar; both groups improved substantially from their preoperative baseline. There was no untreated comparison, so improvement cannot all be attributed to the allocated hormone. Fewer further surgery or second-line-treatment events occurred in the long-acting group, but the confidence interval for that comparison reached the boundary of no difference. Most participants had mild disease, and the cohort was predominantly White. These results support preference-sensitive selection rather than a claim that one strategy universally prevents recurrence. S379

## Surgery, recurrence and preserving choices

Surgery may offer diagnosis, lesion treatment, release of adhesions, removal of an endometrioma or management of threatened organ function. The relevant question is what operation is proposed for what objective. A discussion of "excision" without anatomy, alternatives and expected outcomes is incomplete. For complex deep disease, planning may involve gynecology, colorectal surgery, urology, imaging and fertility specialists. Consent should address the possibility that findings differ from imaging and clarify what additional procedures have or have not been authorized.

Deep surgery can entail bowel, bladder, ureteral, vascular or nerve injury; fistula, anastomotic leak, voiding dysfunction or additional procedures are possible in relevant operations. More extensive surgery is not automatically better for every outcome. Expertise, anatomical mapping, prior operations and the person's priorities matter. An operation that addresses obstruction may have a different justification from one offered mainly for pain. Technical society recommendations are partly based on expert consensus because randomized comparisons are limited. S366

For ovarian endometriomas, reducing pain or recurrence must be balanced against damage to healthy ovarian tissue and ovarian reserve, particularly with bilateral or repeated surgery. Technique recommendations vary with the outcome being prioritized. Cystectomy can be favored over drainage/coagulation for pain and recurrence, while fertility-oriented discussions also consider ablative approaches and reserve. A cyst's size alone does not settle the decision without symptoms, imaging characteristics, reproductive plans and previous surgery. S362 S363

Guidance is not completely uniform. NICE considers a preoperative course of a GnRH agonist before surgery for deep bowel, bladder or ureteric disease. ESHRE advises against preoperative hormonal treatment specifically to improve the immediate surgical pain outcome, while allowing treatment for symptoms during a waiting period. These are partly different questions and evidence interpretations. They should be made explicit in a specialist discussion, not collapsed into a universal rule to start or stop medication before surgery. S362 S363

Postoperative suppression can be discussed when pregnancy is not immediately desired. The decision should consider recurrence prevention, contraception, adverse effects and adherence rather than implying that the operation failed if ongoing medication is offered. Recurrence of symptoms and imaging recurrence are different outcomes. Persistent pain can also have other contributors. Repeat surgery warrants particular care because benefit, adhesion risk and ovarian harm may change after earlier operations. S379

Hysterectomy may be considered in selected circumstances after discussion of alternatives and fertility implications, but it does not guarantee resolution of extrauterine disease or all pain. Removing ovaries is a separate decision with consequences of surgical menopause; it should not be treated as an inevitable component of every hysterectomy. The possibility of continuing symptoms, residual disease and later care should be discussed before an irreversible procedure. S361 S363

## Fertility: choose an outcome before choosing an intervention

Infertility is associated with endometriosis, but neither diagnosis nor disease stage determines an individual's reproductive future. Time trying to conceive, age, ovulation, tubal function, semen factors where relevant, previous surgery and the intended route to parenthood all influence the discussion. Ovarian-reserve testing can help plan treatment but does not give a definitive forecast of natural conception or an assurance that delay is harmless.

Hormonal suppression used for pain is not a treatment to improve pregnancy rates while someone is trying to conceive. Temporary pain control may still fit a person's plans before attempts begin, but its objective and effect on the timing of attempts should be explicit. Fertility assessment should not focus exclusively on endometriosis when other factors may matter. The choice among trying naturally, selected surgery, insemination and assisted reproduction requires an individualized specialist discussion. S363

An older randomized trial in 341 infertile participants with minimal or mild disease found more pregnancies continuing at least 20 weeks after laparoscopic treatment than after diagnostic laparoscopy alone: cumulative probabilities of 30.7% versus 17.7% over the study period. This is evidence for a selected population and outcome, not a guarantee of live birth or support for surgery in every phenotype. It does not answer whether repeated ovarian surgery, deep bowel surgery or surgery before IVF is beneficial for a particular person. S380

Endometrioma surgery before assisted reproduction is not routinely recommended solely to improve live birth; potential ovarian harm and absence of established benefit matter. Pain, concerning features or access to follicles may create different indications. Benefits of deep-lesion surgery for fertility remain uncertain. For extensive ovarian disease or anticipated repeated operations, discuss whether fertility preservation is relevant, while explaining that its eventual benefit is uncertain and that it does not guarantee a child. S363

A practical shared decision should compare consequences of acting now, waiting with monitoring and pursuing fertility treatment first. Document which outcome would cause reconsideration. A person should be able to ask whether a proposed procedure serves pain, organ protection, diagnosis or fertility, and what evidence supports that particular objective.

## Adolescence, menopause and inclusive assessment

Adolescents may have noncyclic pain, nausea or school absence as well as dysmenorrhea. Pain that repeatedly disrupts education or ordinary activities should not be dismissed as a necessary part of puberty. Assessment must account for consent, confidentiality, examination tolerance and possible alternative causes. Adult trial results and medicine labels should not be converted into pediatric prescribing advice. Specialist care is particularly important when initial treatment fails or bone-affecting therapy is considered. S362 S371


Menopause does not make every previous endometriosis symptom irrelevant or every new symptom benign. A new pelvic mass, postmenopausal bleeding or changing symptoms needs assessment for other conditions, including malignancy. Endometriosis can persist after menopause. Menopausal hormone therapy in someone with previous disease requires an individualized discussion; ESHRE favors avoiding estrogen-only regimens because of concern about residual disease, while acknowledging limited evidence. This is not a reason to abruptly change an existing regimen through chat. S363

Evidence and product indications frequently use the term "women," reflecting studied populations and regulatory wording. Endometriosis can also affect trans men and nonbinary people with relevant anatomy. Ask about organs present, surgeries, current hormones, bleeding and pregnancy possibility only as needed and with respectful consent. Gender identity, amenorrhea, testosterone use or a past hysterectomy should not independently close the differential for pelvic pain. Treatment should be coordinated with existing gender-affirming care rather than assuming those medicines must be stopped. Evidence specific to these populations is limited. S361 S364

## Emerging treatments, biomarkers and AI

**Dichloroacetate remains investigational.** EPiC1 was a small single-arm feasibility study, not a randomized demonstration of efficacy. Thirty participants were recruited and 18 completed 12 weeks. Six developed transient peripheral neuropathy and two stopped because of it. The study explored recruitment, retention and tolerability; it cannot establish a cure, improved fertility or a favorable long-term benefit-harm balance. Further controlled evidence is needed. Animal, cell or metabolic findings are not a justification to obtain and self-dose this chemical. S381

**AMY109 is not an available established treatment.** A phase 1 study of an anti-IL-8 antibody included healthy volunteers and a small endometriosis group, primarily assessing safety and pharmacokinetics. It was not powered to establish clinical efficacy or reproductive safety. Moreover, Chugai's 2025 annual report states that in-house AMY109 development was discontinued in July 2025. The report does not establish a single clinical reason for the decision. Older descriptions of an actively progressing program should be updated, and discontinuation should not be misrepresented as proof of a specific toxicity. S382 S383

**Salivary microRNA testing has important new accuracy evidence, with limits.** A 2025 prospective external-validation report included 971 participants and reported sensitivity of 97.3% and specificity of 94.1%. Disease prevalence was 77%, and the reported negative predictive value was 91.3%, not 100%. These figures came from a selected diagnostic population and a sponsor-funded study. They do not directly establish performance as general-population screening, improved patient outcomes, reduced unnecessary surgery or reliable mapping of organ involvement. The abstract, not the full protocol and all supplementary analyses, was inspected here. S384

France introduced access to Endotest in selected centers under an innovation-funding clinical evaluation framework in 2025. This is a jurisdiction-specific pathway, not proof of universal guideline endorsement or availability elsewhere. A biomarker result must be interpreted in the setting in which it was validated. A negative result cannot dismiss an emergency presentation or replace assessment of an alternative disease. Approval or funding status should be checked separately from diagnostic accuracy. S385

AI may assist image interpretation or combine molecular measurements, but an impressive classifier score does not itself establish a useful clinical pathway. Relevant questions include independent external validation, representative participants, prespecified thresholds, missing or indeterminate results, comparison with current practice and actual effects on time to care and outcomes. This packet does not endorse an AI diagnostic tool. Molecular atlases and biomarkers are valuable research advances without being interchangeable with a clinical examination, specialist imaging or a treatment decision. S386 S384

## Follow-up and questions that make care more useful

Follow-up should revisit the original goal and look for benefits, burdens and changes that affect safety. Useful observations include pain days, interference with work or school, sleep, sexual function, bleeding, analgesic use, bowel or bladder symptoms, treatment tolerability and whether pregnancy plans have changed. Monitoring should match the condition and medicine; it is not automatically the same scan or blood panel for everyone.

A person can prepare a short account of the three symptoms that most limit life, previous treatments and why each stopped, existing reports and the main goal for the next phase of care. Useful questions include:

- What findings support the working diagnosis, and what alternatives remain plausible?
- What would a normal scan exclude, and what could it miss?
- Which symptom or organ-related goal is this treatment intended to improve?
- Is this exact product licensed locally, and does it also provide contraception?
- What adverse effects or new symptoms should prompt earlier contact?
- If surgery is proposed, what procedures might be needed and how could they affect ovarian reserve, organs and fertility?
- What improvement would justify continuing, and what would trigger reassessment?

Quality of life belongs in the outcome assessment. A lower pain score with unacceptable bleeding, mood effects or sexual difficulties may not represent an acceptable result for the person taking treatment. Likewise, improved attendance or sleep can matter even if occasional symptoms remain. Agree which observations to track without turning daily life into constant symptom surveillance. Financial barriers, device acceptability, appointment access and the burden of monitoring should be discussed openly because an inaccessible plan is unlikely to meet its intended goal.

A planned review is especially important when symptoms continue despite treatment, new organ symptoms appear, a medication affects bone or mood, or pregnancy intentions change. People with suspected deep bowel, bladder or ureteral disease, endometriomas, or extrapelvic involvement may need specialist follow-up even when pain is currently tolerable. S362

This module is a targeted evidence review, not a systematic review or a validated clinical decision rule. Guidance, approvals and trials answer different questions. The accompanying source register records versions, sections actually inspected and access limitations. Numeric trial results describe studied groups and measured outcomes; they should not be converted into personalized predictions or treatment instructions.
