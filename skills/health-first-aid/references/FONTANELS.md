# Fontanelles, skull shape and head growth: birth to under five

Checked 2026-10-09. General education, urgency recognition and preparation for pediatric assessment. This module does not diagnose craniosynostosis, dehydration, meningitis, rickets or abnormal intracranial pressure, interpret infant images independently, or prescribe supplements. Apply the repository's brief informational disclaimer and local emergency pathway. A normal-looking soft spot, photograph or circumference cannot provide medical clearance.

## First establish urgency

For a fontanel question, establish exact age, gestational age at birth, current alertness, breathing, feeding, urine output, temperature and whether the finding is new. If danger is already described, give the urgent action first. Do not require a photograph, repeated palpation, a fresh circumference or an elective scan before seeking help.

A persistently tense or bulging fontanel when the baby is settled needs prompt medical assessment even if the baby otherwise seems well. Fever, poor responsiveness, a seizure or other signs of illness require emergency assessment. NHS guidance advises urgent assessment for a baby younger than three months with a temperature of at least 38 C. Difficulty waking, abnormal breathing, a first seizure or abnormal responsiveness requires emergency help irrespective of the soft spot. Meningitis can present without every listed symptom; absence of bulging cannot exclude it. These are urgency triggers, not a remote diagnosis. S548 S635 S565

For a well child, distinguish a question about timing from a persistent shape abnormality, concerning growth trajectory or examination finding. Ask what a clinician actually documented. A caregiver being unable to feel the soft spot is different from confirmed closure. Record that uncertainty instead of upgrading the observation into a diagnosis. New symptoms can change a previously nonurgent conversation immediately.

## Anatomy and ordinary care

The newborn skull contains bone plates joined at flexible seams called sutures. A fontanel is a broader membrane-covered space where plates meet. The familiar front soft spot is the anterior fontanel; the smaller one at the back is the posterior fontanel. They are distinct from the sagittal, coronal, metopic and lambdoid sutures. Closing a fontanel does not mean all sutures have fused or that skull growth stops. Premature fusion of a suture can restrict growth in one direction while other regions expand, producing a characteristic shape. S628

Birth molding can produce temporary elongation and overlapping ridges. Persistent or progressive changes need clinical examination rather than indefinite attribution to delivery. Fontanelles have protective tissue and skin; they are not exposed brain or an open hole. Gentle ordinary handling, washing and light touch are generally safe, and visible heartbeat pulsation can be normal. Do not suggest forceful pressure, probing or manual attempts to move a skull seam. A well-infant reassurance about routine handling does not override illness, injury or a new finding. S614

Caregivers can describe location, onset, position and accompanying symptoms. They cannot reliably identify fusion by tracing a ridge. Photos may help communicate shape to a clinician, but cannot assess pressure, palpation, eye movement or neurological function. Do not turn an optional image into a prerequisite for care or provide an image-based exclusion of craniosynostosis.

## Timing is a distribution, not a deadline

The posterior fontanel normally disappears earlier than the anterior fontanel. AAP parent guidance describes posterior closure by about two to three months and anterior closure around eighteen months, before the second birthday. These are approximate orientation points, not mandatory deadlines. An open front fontanel at twelve or fifteen months is not by itself evidence of disease. A small or closed front fontanel during infancy is likewise not a diagnosis. Examine growth, development and shape rather than use the calendar alone. S615

Pindrik and colleagues retrospectively assessed 464 CT scans of full-term infants from birth to twenty-four months. In that sample, 3-5% showed anterior closure at five to six months; more than half were closed at sixteen months, and 88% at twenty months. Median imaged area increased initially before decreasing. The monthly groups were different children selected for imaging, not a population follow-up proving when every child's fontanel should close. These findings illustrate variation; they do not justify screening CT or provide a personal probability of disease. S610

Palpated closure and radiographic ossification are different measurements. A tiny gap can be hard to feel, while imaging can define a different boundary. Study definitions of area, complete closure and contiguous sutures vary. Clinical cohorts differ in gestation, age distribution and why imaging occurred. Explain these differences when reference percentages conflict. Do not present historical medians, old review percentages or population comparisons as a modern universal normal range. Ancestry is not a diagnostic test, and a reported population average cannot clear or label an individual child.

A 2022 Brazilian referral-center comparison of 140 children under thirteen months found that anterior-fontanel closure was a poor stand-alone marker for nonsyndromic craniosynostosis. Fusion could occur with a widely open fontanel, and closure alone did not imply the diagnosis. A larger 2025 retrospective CT comparison found group differences and suggested measurements could assist recognition, but still required clinical evaluation and imaging confirmation. Its ROC cutoffs are research outputs, not parent-facing deadlines or externally validated chat triage. Selected case/control groups cannot estimate prevalence in routine healthy infants. S611 S612

Thus avoid both errors: "closed means fusion" and "open means no fusion." The next action depends on the whole clinical story. A small gap with normal examined shape and stable growth may be followed clinically; a persistent abnormal shape needs assessment even when the gap is open. Do not convert this operating rule into a validated probability engine.

## Head growth: measure, plot and review the trajectory

Obtain dated values, units, exact age, sex-specific chart and gestational age before interpreting a percentile. WHO offers head-circumference-for-age references from birth to five years. Use the local clinical team's chart consistently, and establish its corrected-age approach for prematurity. Do not silently switch charts between visits, apply adult cutoffs or interpret a percentile as a diagnosis. Growth assessment contributes to the overall clinical impression. S616

Head circumference should be measured professionally with suitable equipment and plotted consistently. RCPCH advises trained or supervised measurement, a narrow paper or plastic tape, and accurate recording of measurement dates; age-entry errors can distort plotting. Unexpected results need confirmation. A verified concerning change or persistent shape concern should prompt pediatric assessment using the local pathway, with neurological danger taking priority. This module does not supply one universal centile-crossing or repeat-measurement threshold. Measurement confirmation is not a reason to delay assessment of an acutely ill infant. S634

Ask for actual numbers instead of "normal," "large" or "not growing." Check dates, age entry, tape placement and reference when a surprising change appears, while not dismissing a verified change. A single reading cannot establish growth velocity. Overall circumference and shape are different questions: normal circumference cannot exclude a fused single suture. Review family history and development during clinical assessment rather than assume an explanation from ancestry. S634 S628

Operationally, do not calculate a confident centile from uncertain age or a rounded recalled measurement. Say what remains unverified and what should be examined. Preserve the distinction between a caregiver observation, a professional measurement and an established diagnosis. A follow-up plan should identify who reviews the trajectory, when and which new symptoms mean earlier contact; a new concern need not wait until the next routine birthday visit.

## Bulging, pulsation and a sunken appearance

A fontanel can swell during crying and return flat when crying ends, as described in NSW parent guidance. Ask whether the appearance resolves when the baby settles or remains persistently abnormal. This context is not a home pressure test, and does not clear an unwell infant. Normal heartbeat pulsation can occur. Persistent tension with illness, vomiting, altered alertness or abnormal gaze warrants urgent assessment rather than reassurance from a photograph. S636 S614 S622

Hydrocephalus can involve enlarging head size, tense fontanel, downward-looking eyes, poor feeding, vomiting or sleepiness. These findings do not identify the cause individually. Explain why assessment matters and the immediate action, rather than list catastrophic conditions as equally likely. A closed fontanel does not remove the need to act on concerning neurological symptoms. S622

A sunken appearance is not a stand-alone dehydration diagnosis. Assess the whole infant: feeding, vomiting or diarrhea, urine, responsiveness, mouth moisture, eyes and breathing. NHS guidance identifies fewer wet diapers, reduced tears, sunken eyes or soft spot, and drowsiness or irritability as concerning infant findings requiring urgent advice. Difficulty waking, abnormal breathing, cold skin or concerning color change with dehydration signs needs emergency assessment. Neither a photo nor a normal-looking soft spot proves adequate hydration. Do not advise home intravenous fluids or derive a fluid regimen from fontanel depth. S555

## Positional shape, torticollis and fused sutures

Positional plagiocephaly involves asymmetric flattening from external molding; brachycephaly is more symmetric posterior flattening. Preferred position and restricted neck movement can contribute. Examination considers torticollis, development and other risk factors as well as skull shape. RCH guidance favors clinical diagnosis and usually no imaging when clearly positional, with physiotherapy where appropriate. A persistent unusual shape, suspicious ridge or uncertainty about fusion changes the pathway. Photos and diagram comparisons cannot settle that distinction. S617

Protect safe sleep while addressing shape. Place infants on their backs to sleep on a firm, flat surface; keep pillows and soft bedding out. Do not recommend side or prone sleep, wedges, positioners or head-shaping pillows. Counterpositioning must not undermine this. S619

Awake supervised tummy time and variation in carrying or feeding positions can reduce prolonged pressure. Restricted neck movement merits professional assessment and taught exercises rather than forceful stretching from chat. These measures should not delay referral if fusion is suspected. NHS information describes usual improvement and physiotherapy for tight neck muscles, and does not support routine commercial helmets or headbands for positional flattening. Local specialist practices can differ. S620

Helmet decisions depend on severity, age and the service. Do not promise a cosmetic outcome or sell a helmet as a solution to an undiagnosed shape. A postoperative helmet after endoscopic release has a different purpose from a positional cosmetic helmet. Massage, manipulation and a helmet alone cannot be offered to reopen a fused suture. Parents should ask what diagnosis the proposed device treats, who supervises fit and skin checks, and what benefit is expected. S625

A forehead ridge also needs nuance. Physiological metopic closure can create a ridge with otherwise normal shape; trigonocephaly includes consequential narrowing or triangular forehead and orbital changes. A 2019 retrospective specialist imaging study found that metopic fusion alone was not diagnostic and no single CT feature perfectly separated groups. Its angle measurements are not a home score or image classifier. Persistent narrowing, triangular shape or a ridge accompanied by other changes warrants examination; selected mild findings may be observed after specialist assessment. S621

## A large or late-closing fontanel

Late closure may represent variation, but a persistently large gap deserves pediatric review, especially beyond the usual infancy period or with poor growth, developmental concerns, bone changes or neurological features. Rickets can include late closure, soft skull bones, widened wrists and other bone or growth abnormalities. The gap alone cannot establish vitamin D deficiency. Review diet and the existing prevention plan without choosing treatment doses from chat. S623

Depending on the examination, considerations can include thyroid disease, pressure-related conditions and selected skeletal or genetic disorders. Ask whether newborn screening was completed and its result is known, while recognizing that a screening history does not settle every later concern. A newborn-screening program's parent resource lists a large late-closing fontanel among possible congenital-hypothyroidism features; it is not a diagnostic criterion. Do not generate blanket thyroid, mineral, genetic and scan panels from a gap alone. S626 S622

The practical goal is focused assessment: distinguish a well child with a reviewed variation from a child with a pattern requiring investigation. Explain that the clinician selects tests from history, examination and growth, not because every differential possibility needs exclusion. Avoid alarming lists without context or unverified reassurance that all delayed closure is harmless.

## Vitamin D and calcium are not closure controls

Prevention and treatment of diagnosed deficiency are different purposes. An open or early-closing fontanel is not a reason for chat to start, stop, increase or decrease vitamin D or calcium. Reviewed prevention guidance does not recommend automatically withholding routine prophylaxis because a gap looks small. Preserve a verified existing plan and discuss the examination with the pediatrician unless a clinician has given different instructions or excess exposure is suspected. This is a scope safeguard, not a personalized prescription.

NHS guidance uses UK-specific feeding thresholds and age recommendations. Do not export these as a global regimen, combine different countries' policies, or apply them to a premature infant without its local plan. This module intentionally gives no drop counts or deficiency-treatment schedule. Country, exact preparation and clinician instructions matter. S624

For a label question, obtain ingredient concentration per drop or milliliter, actual amount given, country and all other supplements. NHS warns about duplicates, including cod liver oil plus vitamin drops. Extra drops do not make closure healthier or faster. Suspected overdose needs poison-service or clinical advice using the actual label and amount, not a chat toxicity calculation. Do not use an unverified product photograph to invent concentration. S624

## Imaging and referral answer different questions

History, examination and serial growth review often come first for nonacute concerns. The Dutch multidisciplinary guideline recommends prompt referral when fusion is suspected without delaying it for extra local diagnostics. In its expertise-center pathway, expert skull ultrasound or radiographs can assess moderate suspicion, while low-dose three-dimensional CT addresses high suspicion and planning. This does not mean every small fontanel requires CT. S613

Brain ultrasound through a fontanel examines brain structures and fluid spaces; it is useful in selected concerns such as hydrocephalus or prematurity-related assessment. Dedicated cranial-suture ultrasound asks whether particular seams remain open. "Normal brain ultrasound" should not become "all sutures open" unless the report actually answers that question. Ultrasound does not use ionizing radiation, but expertise, window availability and the indication matter. No reviewed source establishes routine brain ultrasound for every healthy infant. S629 S613

Justified CT should use pediatric radiation-minimizing protocols; do not categorically reject necessary imaging. MRI has no ionizing radiation but can require sedation or anesthesia for longer studies, while some rapid protocols do not. Invasive pressure tests belong to specialist decisions. Ask what each test changes and whether an appropriate alternative exists. Do not infer pressure, diagnosis or surgical eligibility from a displayed slice. S613

## Treatment and follow-up through preschool age

Confirmed care can involve observation in selected mild presentations, endoscopic release with postoperative helmeting, open reconstruction and continued follow-up. Choices and age windows depend on suture, severity, associated conditions, age and center. Early referral preserves options; do not guarantee a technique by a particular birthday. Prepare questions about anesthesia, blood loss, infection, additional procedures and monitoring rather than estimate individual risks. S627 S625

Closure does not end surveillance for a child with a craniofacial condition. Into toddler and preschool years, care may include vision, hearing, breathing and developmental review. New headache, vomiting, visual change or loss of skills merits reassessment even after surgery. Conversely, a reviewed variation in a normally developing child does not automatically require repeated scans or laboratory panels simply because the gap was once small or large. S627

Prepare a concise visit agenda: concern and onset; dated growth results and chart; gestational age; feeding and supplement labels; development; and existing examination and scan reports. Ask who follows growth, whether referral is needed, what imaging would answer and which changes mean earlier contact. This documentation aid is not a new testing schedule. Keep uncertainty and provenance visible throughout.
