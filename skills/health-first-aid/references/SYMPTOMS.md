# Symptom and specialty decision-entry navigation

Checked 2026-10-09. Original public educational content. This supports care navigation and discussion with a clinician; it does not diagnose, prescribe or clear an injury. Danger recognition identifies a reason to obtain assessment, not a confirmed disease.

## How to use these cards

Select the symptom the person describes, then screen for an immediate danger before asking a long history. Ask only information that changes the next step: age group, onset and trajectory, current severity/function, key associated symptoms, pregnancy possibility, relevant illness/medicines and an existing treatment or discharge plan. A person may enter several cards: abdominal pain with bleeding follows the bleeding urgency, while a cough with severe breathlessness follows the breathing urgency. Missing details should prompt a focused question, not an invented normal finding.

The most important update is what has changed. An earlier benign explanation, normal-looking photo, negative home test or temporary improvement must be revisited if new symptoms appear. A recent focal weakness or speech disturbance remains urgent after it resolves. Record when the episode started and when the person was last at their usual baseline; do not spend emergency time building a diagnostic score. S487

Use three proportionate outputs: emergency services/emergency department now when immediate danger is described; urgent assessment today or through a local triage service when a time-sensitive problem may need examination; planned primary-care/pharmacy review or brief observation when the pattern is mild, stable and without warning features. These categories are navigation language, not a validated triage instrument. Clinical services may appropriately change urgency after examination. UK sources describe UK 111/999 and specific pharmacist eligibility. Refer to local emergency services and available care pathways elsewhere; do not export UK numbers or prescribing permissions by default.

Home observation means noticing ordinary function, trend, fluid tolerance, urine/stool and symptoms already present. It does not mean pressing deeply on the abdomen, manipulating joints/testicles, forcing neck movement, standing repeatedly to trigger a faint, testing pupils or performing an expert neurological manoeuvre remotely. Optional non-intimate photographs can support a rash description, healing trend or label identification in nonurgent care; they cannot exclude deep infection, fracture, stroke or sight-threatening disease. Never ask for intimate images and never postpone urgent help to obtain media.

Pregnancy/postpartum context, very young children, frailty, immunosuppression, anticoagulation, recent surgery and a clinician's disease-specific rescue plan alter the threshold. Ask for the exact relevant plan. An adult fever page is not an infant fever rule. A younger age does not clear dangerous chest, eye or neurological symptoms. Medicines can cause symptoms, but attributing a change to them does not settle its urgency; arrange review rather than changing essential treatment from a bare symptom description. Follow exact verified urgent product-safety instructions, dispatcher/clinician directions or an existing prescribed rescue plan when dangerous exposure is suspected; this boundary must not delay emergency help or force continued exposure. Do not invent a replacement or restart regimen. S471 S368 S476

Each response should state the next action, why the reported feature changes that action, and what worsening would require earlier help. A useful reassurance is conditional: the currently described mild pattern can be observed while it improves, with named escalation triggers and a review point. Avoid long catastrophic differentials when a focused safety check and practical advice suffice. Conversely, a calm tone must not minimize substantial bleeding, acute visual loss, severe pain or evolving neurological symptoms.

## Decision cards

### Headache

**Decision-changing context.** Is this familiar or a first/change in pattern? Establish sudden versus gradual onset, injury, fever, vomiting, vision changes, pregnancy/postpartum status and any weakness, speech or walking change.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Tension-type headache, migraine, missed meals and dehydration are common possibilities. Sudden extreme pain or neurological change raises concern for a vascular or other intracranial emergency; fever with stiffness or light intolerance raises concern for infection.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency assessment for abrupt extreme headache, new neurological symptoms, confusion, seizure, loss of vision or headache after relevant head injury. Same-day assessment for headache with eye symptoms, vomiting, cough/exertion triggers, jaw pain when chewing or scalp tenderness. Familiar mild headache without these features can be observed with regular meals, usual fluids, rest and a diary; arrange review if worsening or recurrent.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Record onset and what changed without repeatedly provoking pain. Do not call a new neurological episode 'just migraine' because an earlier migraine was benign. S458 S487

### Fever and feeling acutely unwell

**Decision-changing context.** Age and baseline vulnerability matter before interpreting temperature. Ask duration, reliable measurement, drinking/urine, breathing, alertness, rash, focal pain, medicines and a prescribed immunosuppression or postoperative emergency plan.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** A respiratory or other self-limited infection is common. Severe systemic infection or a focal infection remains possible when the person deteriorates; a modest or absent measured fever cannot erase confusion, breathing difficulty or serious pain.

**Next step and safety net.** A stable adult who drinks and breathes normally can rest and observe the trend; seek urgent assessment if home care fails or symptoms worsen. Confusion, severe breathlessness or fever accompanying severe headache with neck stiffness needs emergency routing. Children need age-specific guidance rather than the adult threshold. Immunosuppression, pregnancy and frailty require a lower threshold and any existing fever plan.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Report general condition as well as the number. Cooling a reading or taking a fever medicine is not proof the cause is harmless. S459 S458 S469 S471

### Chest pain or pressure

**Decision-changing context.** Ask whether discomfort is present now, onset, duration, radiation, breathlessness, sweating, nausea, faintness, exertion, recent injury and cardiovascular history. Do not wait to finish this list when emergency features are already present.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Reflux, chest muscle strain and anxiety are possible. Cardiac ischaemia, inflammatory heart disease or lung disease are important alternatives; burning, young age or an anxiety history does not independently exclude a dangerous cause.

**Next step and safety net.** Call local emergency services for persistent sudden discomfort, spreading pain or chest pain with sweating, nausea, lightheadedness or breathlessness. Recurrent or quickly resolved unexplained pain still needs medical review; resolved episodes with concerning associated symptoms warrant urgent advice. Mild clearly improving discomfort after a minor strain may be monitored only when danger features are absent.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Rest while arranging help and do not drive with concerning symptoms. Do not use an antacid response, pressing on the chest or a wearable trace as a clearance test. S460 S469 S467

### Abdominal pain

**Decision-changing context.** Locate pain and timing, severity and change, last stool/gas and urine, vomiting, blood, fever, pregnancy possibility, recent surgery and medicines. Ask about ability to eat, drink and carry out usual activity.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Gas, indigestion, gastroenteritis and constipation are common explanations. Appendicitis, obstruction, gallstone or kidney-stone complications and bleeding can initially overlap with these descriptions.

**Next step and safety net.** Sudden or severe pain, collapse, inability to pass urine or stool/gas, blood vomit or black sticky stool needs emergency evaluation. Worsening or persistent pain, pregnancy, unusual vaginal bleeding or urinary change needs urgent assessment. Mild improving symptoms may be observed with fluids as tolerated and a record of intake, output and location; recurrent symptoms need planned review.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Avoid repeated deep pressing, jumping or deliberate symptom-provocation to 'test appendicitis'. A prior IBS diagnosis is not permission to ignore a new severe pattern. S461

### Pelvic pain and pregnancy possibility

**Decision-changing context.** Clarify current reproductive anatomy without assumptions, pregnancy possibility and timing, period/bleeding pattern, discharge, urinary/bowel symptoms, fever and pain during sex. Ask only relevant sensitive details and explain their purpose.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Period pain, constipation, bladder symptoms and chronic pelvic conditions are possibilities. Ectopic pregnancy, severe pelvic infection, appendicitis and other acute organ problems require consideration when context changes.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency assessment for severe/worsening pain, faintness, shoulder-tip pain, breathing difficulty or heavy vaginal bleeding. Pelvic pain with possible pregnancy, fever, unusual discharge or urinary/bowel difficulty needs urgent assessment. Stable recurrent pain merits planned primary-care review, with gynaecology, urology or gastrointestinal referral according to findings.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Record cycle relationship and functional effect. Do not wait for a home pregnancy test result to escalate collapse or severe pain, and do not request intimate photographs. S368

### Nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea

**Decision-changing context.** Ask age, onset, frequency, fluid tolerance, last urine/wet nappies, blood or green vomit, abdominal pain, poisoning possibility and relevant illness. Separate mild nausea from repeated vomiting that prevents hydration.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** A stomach infection or food-related illness is common. Dehydration, obstruction, poisoning or another acute abdominal or neurological cause may produce similar symptoms.

**Next step and safety net.** Blood/coffee-ground vomit, green vomit, severe abdominal pain, severe headache with stiff neck/light sensitivity, confusion or severe breathing difficulty needs emergency care. Urgent advice for inability to retain fluids, bloody diarrhoea, reduced urine, a baby feeding poorly, or prolonged vomiting/diarrhoea. Stable people can take small frequent sips and eat as tolerated; pharmacy advice can help select an appropriate oral rehydration product.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Track fluid retained and urine rather than only stool counts. Do not dilute infant formula or use adult anti-diarrhoeal medicines in children. Avoid a blanket medication recommendation before context is known. S462

### Constipation and altered bowel habit

**Decision-changing context.** Compare with usual pattern; ask stool consistency, straining, duration, gas, abdominal swelling/pain, vomiting, blood, weight loss, new medicines and mobility. Watery leakage can coexist with retained stool.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Routine change, low intake/activity and medicine effects are common. Impaction, obstruction or an underlying bowel disorder matters when pain, vomiting, inability to pass gas or a persistent unexplained change appears.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency evaluation for severe abdominal symptoms or inability to pass gas with pain/vomiting. Persistent constipation, blood, weight loss or sudden bowel-habit change needs medical review. Without danger features, gradually adjust fibre, usual fluid intake, movement and toilet routine; a pharmacist can help if these fail.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Do not manually remove stool, use repeated enemas from chat, or stop prescribed medicines unilaterally. Do not mistake leakage diarrhoea for proof constipation has resolved. S463 S461

### Blood in stool or vomit

**Decision-changing context.** Ask where blood appeared, amount and ongoing versus stopped bleeding, colour/texture, clots, abdominal pain, dizziness, weakness and anticoagulants. Clarify food/medicine colour effects without assuming they explain bleeding.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** A fissure or haemorrhoid can cause small bright-red streaks. Gastrointestinal bleeding, inflammatory disease and malignancy are alternatives; appearance alone cannot establish the source.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency care for non-stop or substantial bleeding, large clots, blood/coffee-ground vomit, collapse, or concerning black sticky stool with abdominal symptoms. Black/dark-red stool and bloody diarrhoea need urgent assessment. A small isolated streak without systemic symptoms can receive proportionate advice, but recurrence, bowel-habit change, pain, fatigue or weight loss needs review.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Record an ordinary description; a photo is optional only when safe and non-intimate. Never reassure severe bleeding because haemorrhoids or anticoagulants seem an available explanation. S464 S462 S461

### Dizziness, vertigo, imbalance and fainting

**Decision-changing context.** Distinguish spinning, near-fainting and imbalance; ask sudden onset, persistence, standing relationship, hearing symptoms, medicines, hydration and new vision, speech, weakness or walking changes. If consciousness was lost, ask recovery, exercise/lying-down onset, injury and witnesses.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Dehydration, postural symptoms, medicine effects and inner-ear problems are common. Neurological or cardiac disease can also present this way and cannot be excluded by a spinning description. A blackout during exercise or while lying down may have a cardiac cause.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency routing for sudden focal neurological symptoms, including a recent episode that resolved. Persistent or recurrent dizziness needs medical review; accompanying hearing/vision changes, headache, palpitations or collapse change the route. Mild passing dizziness without danger features can be observed while resting and getting up slowly. Fainting with incomplete recovery, cardiac symptoms, serious injury or seizure-like activity requires emergency help; recovered fainting still needs clinical assessment. Use the emergency module for unresponsiveness.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Do not drive, climb or operate machinery while dizzy. Record triggers without repeatedly standing to reproduce symptoms. Do not perform a remote HINTS, pupil or provocative positional examination as a stroke exclusion test. Do not drive pending clinical advice after blackout. S465 S487 S466

### Racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat

**Decision-changing context.** Ask present versus resolved, duration, recurrence, exercise relation, chest pain, breathlessness, faintness, known heart disease, family history, stimulants and new medicines.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Stress, poor sleep, caffeine and medicine effects are common explanations. Anaemia, thyroid disease and rhythm disorders can also cause palpitations.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency care for ongoing palpitations that do not settle or associated chest pain, breathlessness or faintness. If the concerning associated episode has stopped, obtain urgent assessment rather than returning to routine reassurance. Recurring, longer or increasingly frequent episodes need review, particularly with heart history.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Record timing and an available pulse/wearable observation without treating it as a diagnosis or exclusion. Reducing avoidable stimulants can be reasonable; do not change prescribed rhythm or blood-pressure medicines from a bare reading. S467

### Fatigue and reduced stamina

**Decision-changing context.** Ask duration and functional change, sleep/snoring, mood, diet, medicines, infection, weight change, thirst/urination, bleeding, breathlessness and pregnancy context. Distinguish chronic fatigue from sudden collapse or weakness.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Sleep disruption, stress and recovery from illness are common. Anaemia, diabetes, thyroid disease, sleep apnoea and other illness may need targeted assessment rather than a generic supplement or test panel.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency features follow their own routes: chest symptoms, severe breathlessness, confusion or focal weakness must not be parked under fatigue. Arrange primary-care review when unexplained symptoms persist for weeks, impair daily life or accompany weight change or other symptoms. Brief fatigue after sleep loss can be observed with a realistic sleep and activity record.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Use a diary to describe capacity and recovery, not a challenge to push through disabling symptoms. Discuss targeted tests after history/examination; do not prescribe iron or hormones based on tiredness alone. S468 S469 S487

### Breathlessness

**Decision-changing context.** Assess ability to speak and usual function before detailed questions. Ask onset, at rest versus activity, chest symptoms, fever, wheeze, leg swelling, recent operation/immobility and existing respiratory rescue plan.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Asthma, infection and panic can cause breathlessness; cardiac or serious lung disease may have similar descriptions. An anxiety history does not settle a new episode.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency help for gasping, inability to speak, heavy/tight chest, spreading pain, marked colour change or sudden confusion; a child with grunting or chest recession needs urgent emergency routing. New or worse breathlessness needs timely assessment; associated leg pain/swelling or blood in sputum heightens concern. Progressive activity limitation or breathlessness lying flat needs planned medical review without indefinite observation.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Follow an already prescribed rescue plan while arranging appropriate help. Do not make a home oxygen reading the sole clearance criterion or introduce new inhaler/oxygen dosing. S469

### Cough and coughing blood

**Decision-changing context.** Ask duration, deterioration, breathing, fever, chest pain, actual blood amount, immune status, smoking, medicines and impact on sleep/activity.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Viral illness, reflux, allergy and postnasal symptoms are common. Pneumonia and other lung illness require consideration when severe, persistent or accompanied by systemic features.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency features use the breathlessness/chest route. Coughing blood, difficult breathing, chest pain, feeling very unwell or rapidly worsening cough needs urgent assessment. A cough beyond three weeks, unexplained weight loss or immunosuppression merits review. A mild improving cough can be observed with rest, usual fluids and smoke avoidance.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Document change from baseline. Antibiotics are not routine for a cough; mucus colour alone does not establish bacterial infection. Ask a pharmacist about symptom products and age/pregnancy suitability rather than recommending a combination cold remedy automatically. S470 S469

### Burning, frequency or urgency when urinating

**Decision-changing context.** Ask anatomy, pregnancy, age, duration, fever/rigors, flank pain, visible blood, discharge or relevant sexual exposure, catheter, immune status and ability to pass urine.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Bladder infection is possible; urethral/genital irritation, STI or other urinary conditions can overlap. Fever/flank pain or systemic deterioration raises concern beyond an uncomplicated bladder episode.

**Next step and safety net.** Urgent assessment for suspected infection in pregnancy, children, older/frail people, male urinary anatomy, immunosuppression, catheter use, flank pain, blood or worsening symptoms. Confusion/drowsiness or speech difficulty needs emergency help. Mild uncomplicated symptoms require locally appropriate pharmacy/primary-care advice, not automatic antibiotics from chat.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Observe frequency, urine output and associated symptoms. Dark or smelly urine alone is insufficient to diagnose infection. Do not infer a UTI as the sole explanation for new confusion or use leftover antibiotics. S471

### Visible urinary blood and retention

**Decision-changing context.** Clarify whether urine itself is discoloured, first versus repeated episode, clots, pain, fever, last void, bladder discomfort and relevant medicines/procedure. Ask about possible vaginal contamination without dismissing the report.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Infection, stones and prostate problems can cause blood or voiding difficulty. Obstruction and serious urinary disease need assessment; an anticoagulant or plausible food colour is not a complete explanation.

**Next step and safety net.** Visible or suspected urinary blood needs urgent medical review even once or without pain. Sudden inability to pass urine, particularly with abdominal pain, needs emergency assessment. Fever/flank pain or deterioration follows the infection route; neurological symptoms with retention follow the back-pain emergency route.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Record output and timing. Do not force large fluid volumes against retention, attempt catheter insertion or advise stopping anticoagulants. Urology referral and tests depend on examination and findings. S472 S461 S471 S486

### Testicular pain, swelling or lump

**Decision-changing context.** Ask onset, severity, persistence at rest, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, trauma, fever, urinary symptoms and new swelling/lump. Keep questioning brief when sudden pain is reported.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Infection, injury, hernia or cysts are possibilities. Testicular torsion threatens tissue and cannot be safely ruled out by chat, age, a photograph or a guessed infection.

**Next step and safety net.** Sudden severe pain, pain with nausea/vomiting/abdominal pain, or pain persisting over an hour or at rest needs immediate emergency assessment. New lump, swelling or concerning ache needs urgent clinical review. Do not wait overnight to see whether severe pain becomes less embarrassing to discuss.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Report onset and accompanying symptoms without manipulation. No intimate photos, self-twisting, lifting manoeuvres or remote clearance. A pain-only presentation is not automatically cancer, and a painless lump still needs assessment. S168

### Ear pain and discharge

**Decision-changing context.** Ask age, one or both ears, duration, fever/general condition, discharge, swelling, hearing change, foreign object, trauma and tooth/throat symptoms.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Ear infection, wax or pressure problems are possible; dental or throat disease can refer pain. Swelling and systemic illness alter the urgency rather than proving a simple middle-ear infection.

**Next step and safety net.** Mild earache can be observed briefly; seek assessment if lasting more than two to three days or sooner with fever, illness, swelling, discharge, hearing change or a foreign object. Young children, particularly bilateral symptoms under two years, need appropriate clinical advice. Sudden hearing loss follows the separate urgent route.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Keep the ear dry and avoid cotton buds or attempts to remove a lodged object/wax. A photograph cannot inspect the middle ear or justify drops when perforation is possible; product choice belongs with a pharmacist/clinician. S473 S474

### Sudden or progressive hearing loss

**Decision-changing context.** Identify sudden versus gradual and one versus both ears, days/weeks of worsening, tinnitus, vertigo, pain/discharge, noise exposure or injury. Ask what the person can no longer hear compared with baseline.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Wax, infection and eardrum injury can affect hearing; age/noise-related change is often gradual. A sudden loss can need rapid treatment and must not be assumed to be wax.

**Next step and safety net.** Sudden hearing loss, rapidly worsening hearing or hearing loss with pain/discharge needs urgent assessment and access to ENT as appropriate. Gradual persistent loss needs a hearing assessment, including after treatment when hearing has not returned. Neurological symptoms with new dizziness/hearing change require their emergency route.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Use quieter settings and written communication while arranging help. Do not delay urgent assessment for an online hearing test, home wax extraction or empiric ear drops. S474 S473 S487

### Sore throat and swallowing difficulty

**Decision-changing context.** Ask ability to breathe, swallow fluids/saliva and speak, rapid change, fever, dehydration, age, immune status and persistent lump or ulcer. Do not request an intrusive throat examination.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Viral irritation is common; bacterial infection and more serious airway or deep infection can overlap. Severity and airway function matter more than a photograph's apparent redness.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency help for breathing difficulty, inability to swallow, drooling, stridor or severe rapidly worsening symptoms. Urgent advice for dehydration, marked fever or immunosuppression. Mild symptoms can use fluids, soft foods and locally suitable pharmacy advice; review if not improving after a week or recurrent. A persistent mouth/neck lump or ulcer needs assessment.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Do not force food, drink or a deep throat inspection when swallowing is unsafe. Antibiotics are not routinely needed and should follow clinical evaluation rather than a colour or white-spot rule. S475

### Nosebleed and sinus/facial symptoms

**Decision-changing context.** For bleeding ask duration, amount, trauma, dizziness, breathing and anticoagulation. For congestion ask course, fever, unilateral/recurrent symptoms, immune status and associated eye/neurological symptoms.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Dryness and minor irritation commonly bleed; clotting problems, medicines and injury modify risk. Post-viral or allergic congestion is common; worsening illness or eye/neurological involvement cannot be reduced to sinus pressure.

**Next step and safety net.** Nosebleed lasting beyond ten to fifteen minutes, excessive bleeding, vomiting swallowed blood, head injury, weakness/dizziness or breathing difficulty needs emergency-department assessment. For mild bleeding use existing verified first-aid guidance. Sinus symptoms that worsen, cause marked illness or affect an immunosuppressed person need urgent advice; persistence/recurrent or unilateral symptoms merits clinical review.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Track course; do not tilt back or insert objects. Avoid automatic antibiotics for coloured nasal mucus. Sudden visual loss, photophobia or neurological symptoms follows the appropriate emergency route. S476 S477 S478 S487

### Red eye, eye pain and contact lenses

**Decision-changing context.** Ask pain, light sensitivity, any visual change, headache/nausea, contact lenses, trauma/chemical exposure, recent surgery and age. Do not use photographs to decide that vision is safe.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Dryness, conjunctivitis and a surface blood spot may be relatively minor. Infection, inflammation or other sight-threatening disease must be considered with pain, lenses, light intolerance or changed vision.

**Next step and safety net.** Urgent same-day advice for a painful red eye or red eye in a contact-lens wearer; newborn redness also needs urgent assessment. Redness with visual change, photophobia, severe headache/nausea or injury needs emergency eye assessment. A painless eye with normal vision and no illness may receive brief observation/pharmacy advice, but persistence merits review.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Stop contact-lens wear; avoid rubbing. Do not prescribe steroid, anaesthetic or leftover antibiotic drops, test pupils or reassure from a clear-looking image. S478

### Vision loss, flashes and floaters

**Decision-changing context.** Ask sudden versus longstanding, one/both eyes, loss or blurring, curtain/shadow, first/new/increasing floaters, pain, injury or recent eye surgery. Timing matters even if symptoms improve.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Longstanding unchanged floaters can accompany benign vitreous change. New floaters/flashes can also accompany retinal tear/detachment; sudden visual loss can have other serious causes.

**Next step and safety net.** Arrange urgent eye assessment for first or sudden/increasing flashes or floaters, especially with blurred vision, pain or after surgery/injury. A curtain, shadow or sudden loss of sight needs immediate assessment. If a neurological episode is suspected, use emergency stroke routing even when it has resolved. Stable familiar floaters without change can be discussed in routine care.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Describe what changed without prolonged visual self-testing. Absence of pain does not rule out a retinal problem. No pupil checks or reliance on a photograph to exclude disease. S480 S478 S487

### Eye trauma or chemical exposure

**Decision-changing context.** Establish product/exposure and time, high-speed impact or piercing, pain and vision change. Ask minimal questions while arranging urgent action. Package information is useful if safely available.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Minor dust/shampoo irritation may settle. Strong chemical injury, penetrating injury or high-speed fragments can threaten sight despite a modest external appearance.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency assessment for strong chemical exposure, a pierced eye, high-speed impact, changed vision, severe pain or light sensitivity. For chemical exposure, begin gentle clean-water rinsing immediately and continue while arranging help; the inspected guidance specifies at least twenty minutes. Do not delay irrigation to locate a special solution or photograph.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Do not remove a penetrating object, rub the eye or put contact lenses back in. Bring the container if safe. Persistent minor injury after a day needs clinical advice; chat cannot clear an injury. S479

### Rash, itch and sudden swelling

**Decision-changing context.** Ask onset/spread, itch versus pain, illness/fever, new products/medicines, mouth/eye symptoms, breathing/swallowing, pregnancy, immune status and skin appearance across the person's usual tone.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Dryness, irritation, eczema, allergy and infection can look alike. Rapid swelling with airway symptoms may signal anaphylaxis; a severely unwell person with rash needs urgent evaluation regardless of appearance.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency help for sudden mouth/tongue/throat swelling, breathing or swallowing difficulty, faintness or confusion after a possible allergen. Use an existing prescribed rescue plan and emergency module. New severe or widespread itch, recurring rash, pregnancy-associated itch or symptoms impairing life need clinical review; mild isolated irritation can use bland unperfumed moisturiser and avoid new irritants.

A spreading painful, blistering or peeling rash, especially with fever or sores/swelling in the mouth or eyes after starting a medicine, needs immediate emergency assessment. Serious drug reactions can threaten skin, organs and sight; bring medicine names/packages if safely available. Do not wait for photos or an antihistamine response. The emergency team must review suspected causative medicines, with exact product safety instructions applied while help is arranged. S488

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Optional non-intimate photos can document spread and labels; do not delay care or infer harmlessness from blanching/colour. Antihistamine response does not exclude serious disease, and every red spot is not cancer. S481 S177 S458

### Wounds and postoperative changes

**Decision-changing context.** Ask mechanism, depth if known, ongoing bleeding, contamination/bite, sensation/movement, fever, increasing pain/redness/swelling and procedure/date. Obtain the actual discharge warnings and surgical contact route rather than inventing a universal recovery timetable.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** A small superficial wound may heal with cleaning and covering. Infection, deep injury, bleeding or a procedure-specific complication requires review; 'expected postoperative pain' must be reconsidered when the trajectory changes.

**Next step and safety net.** Emergency assessment for uncontrolled bleeding, deep/large wounds, retained objects or loss of sensation/movement. Urgent advice for increasing painful redness, pus, fever, dirty wounds or bites. After surgery, exact discharge instructions prevail; worsening symptoms should reach the treating team promptly, with emergency chest/breath/bleeding routes overriding routine contact delays.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** For a minor wound after bleeding stops, rinse and cover with a clean dressing. Do not remove embedded objects, reopen a surgical wound or obtain exercise/weight-bearing clearance from photos. S482 S460 S469

### Lumps, hair/nail change and local swelling

**Decision-changing context.** Ask site, duration, growth, pain/heat/redness, recurrence, fever, nearby infection, medicines and effect on function. For hair/nails describe sudden/gradual or local/widespread change and associated skin symptoms.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Cysts, lipomas and reactive glands are common lump explanations. Abscess or serious growth remains possible when enlarging, hard/fixed or persistent. Hair/nail appearance alone rarely settles the cause; use the deeper dermatology/ageing route for assessment.

**Next step and safety net.** A growing, hard/fixed, recurrent or persistent lump, or breast/testicular lump, needs clinical assessment rather than image classification. Red/hot painful swelling with fever needs urgent advice. Nonurgent hair/nail changes can be documented and discussed in routine care; pain, spreading inflammation or systemic illness changes the route.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Measure change without squeezing or cutting. Optional non-intimate photos and product/medicine labels may improve description. Do not claim every red growth is cancer or a soft mobile lump is definitively benign. S483 S481 S482

### Joint inflammation, injury and possible fracture

**Decision-changing context.** Ask injury mechanism/time, pain/function, swelling, deformity, sensation/colour/temperature, fever, morning stiffness, multiple joints and any postoperative restrictions. Avoid repeated forced movement or weight-bearing tests.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Overuse, sprain, osteoarthritis and inflammatory arthritis are possibilities. A hot swollen joint may be infected; trauma may cause a fracture or dislocation even if a photo looks normal.

**Next step and safety net.** Hot swollen joints or joint pain with fever/general illness needs urgent assessment. Severe traumatic pain, inability to bear weight, deformity, sensory change or a cold/colour-changed limb needs emergency assessment. Persistent functional limitation, recurrent swelling or prolonged morning stiffness needs medical review. Mild improving injury without these features can be protected from aggravating activity while seeking locally suitable symptom advice.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Do not manipulate a joint into place, stress-test it or clear return to sport remotely. Imaging and rehabilitation decisions require appropriate assessment; postoperative instructions determine movement/weight-bearing limits. S484 S485

### Back/neck pain and neurological red flags

**Decision-changing context.** Ask trauma, sudden/severe or progressive pattern, fever/illness, cancer/immune history, limb weakness/numbness, walking, bladder/bowel change and altered genital/saddle sensation. For neck symptoms avoid forced motion or manipulation.

**Alternatives and uncertainty.** Strain and mechanical pain are common; nerve compression, fracture, infection or other serious disease becomes relevant with neurological/systemic changes.

**Next step and safety net.** Back pain with bilateral leg symptoms, saddle/genital sensory loss or new bladder/bowel/sexual-function change needs immediate emergency assessment. Serious trauma or chest symptoms also warrants emergency routing. Sudden severe worsening pain or fever/general illness needs urgent advice. Persistent activity-limiting or unexplained night/weight-loss symptoms needs review. Mild uncomplicated improving pain may use tolerable ordinary movement rather than prolonged bed rest.

**Safe observations and boundaries.** Do not ask intimate photos or perform strength/reflex/pupil manoeuvres as clearance. New retention overrides a benign strain label; stop aggravating exercises and seek assessment when the pattern changes. S486 S487

## Choosing a specialty without losing the first step

Primary care usually coordinates an unclear stable presentation. Emergency or urgent assessment comes before routine specialty booking when danger features are present. Neurology is a possible route for recurrent headache or unexplained neurological symptoms after initial assessment; acute focal deficits use emergency services. Cardiology may assess recurrent palpitations/blackouts, while a chest-pain emergency is not a reason to wait for an outpatient cardiologist. Gastroenterology may assess recurrent bowel symptoms, unexplained bleeding or persistent digestive symptoms once immediate risks are addressed. Urology may investigate urinary blood/retention or recurrent urinary symptoms. Gynaecology and maternity services address pelvic or pregnancy-related symptoms according to urgency. ENT/audiology supports hearing/ear or persistent unilateral sinus concerns; ophthalmology/urgent eye services handle time-sensitive sight symptoms. Dermatology supports persistent unexplained skin, hair or nail problems, while infected wounds and systemic illness may need general urgent assessment. Rheumatology or musculoskeletal/rehabilitation services may help inflammatory or persistent joint/back problems after urgent infection, fracture and neurological risks have been considered. These are routing possibilities, not mandatory referral lists or a test package. S484 S474 S483

Treatment discussion should follow the likely clinical question. Explain that antibiotics can be useful for an assessed bacterial infection and do not routinely speed every cough, sore throat or sinus episode. Imaging, urine/stool tests, ECG and blood tests may be selected after history/examination; no universal panel belongs to a symptom title. Pain relief and pharmacy products depend on age, pregnancy, comorbidity, interacting medicines and the exact formulation/label. This module intentionally offers no prescription, individual dose or taper. Existing prescribed rescue instructions and verified lay first aid belong to their separate modules. S470 S475 S477

## Evidence limits and maintenance

These cards paraphrase inspected institutional patient-care pages. They are not a systematic review, sensitivity/specificity estimate, diagnostic probability calculator or clinically validated autonomous triage tool.
