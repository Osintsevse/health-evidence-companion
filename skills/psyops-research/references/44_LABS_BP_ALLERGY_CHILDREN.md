## L2 — A reference interval, clinical decision limit and urgent alert answer different questions

**Scope:** conceptual laboratory literacy, without analyte-specific cutoffs or a test panel.

A reference interval describes results in a defined reference population under specified conditions. A clinical decision limit addresses a particular diagnostic, risk or management decision. A critical-result alert calls for prompt action under the laboratory's communication policy. They are not interchangeable labels for a boundary between health and disease. Not every test uses a two-sided interval. [S685, S686]

Interpret the reported interval with the population, method, specimen and preanalytical context. A result inside it can still matter, including a relevant change from prior results; a flag outside it does not alone establish disease. A decision threshold may differ from the printed interval. An urgent message from the laboratory deserves prompt clinical follow-up even if an online reference range looks reassuring. [S685, S686]

**Original fictional example:** a report flags one result slightly outside its interval, while another remains inside but has changed over time. Clarify exact test, units, specimen, method and dates, then the clinical question and whether a decision limit, meaningful trend or urgent communication applies. Neither flag alone answers the whole question. Do not invent a universal critical value.

These concepts do not replace current analyte-specific guidelines, full standards or local critical-result policy. Current critical-communication guidance remains further study.

## B2 — Confirm the pattern, then ask what an investigation would clarify

**Scope:** adult education using US sources; pregnancy, children, acute illness and individual treatment need separate pathways.

The USPSTF 2021 screening recommendation applies to adults aged 18 or older without known hypertension. It recommends office screening followed by appropriate validated ambulatory or home measurements outside the clinical setting to confirm the diagnosis before treatment. This does not make every home device, sampling schedule or threshold suitable everywhere. [S205]

NHLBI describes repeated measurements, office-only and masked patterns, and assessment of history and possible causes or organ effects. Investigations should answer relevant questions about risk, an underlying condition or consequences; this educational page does not establish a universal baseline panel. Confirmation and evaluation are different tasks. [S684]

**Original fictional example:** several high office readings lead a person to ask whether the diagnosis is settled and which blood tests are mandatory. Explain the need for applicable confirmation and why the clinician's history, examination and question guide testing. Do not apply a US threshold to another jurisdiction or diagnose the vignette.

**Reading and limits:** USPSTF official recommendation/practice considerations and evidence-gap sections read by researcher; root checked population, confirmation, implementation and source notice. NHLBI diagnosis page, updated 2025-06-26, read by researcher and relevant sections rechecked by root. The USPSTF recommendation is independent of US government policy. Detailed ESC/WHO investigation recommendations were not read from inaccessible PDFs or contents headings. Current Serbian/Russian confirmation, exact investigations and special-population guidance remain gaps.

## AR1 — Identify the test and the exposure question before interpreting a positive result

**Scope:** IgE blood-test literacy and separately scoped food-allergy evidence; no test-selection algorithm or home challenge.

Total IgE does not identify a particular allergen. Allergen-specific IgE concerns a named target, but a positive result alone does not establish the cause or severity of a person's symptoms. False positive and false negative results are possible; the inspected consumer page does not establish a universal error rate. Interpret results with symptoms, exposure history and the purpose of testing. [S690]

For food allergy, FDA distinguishes sensitization from a reproducible adverse reaction on exposure. Its 2025 public-health evidence framework considers both sensitization and reactivity; it is a regulatory framework for evaluating non-listed food allergens, not an individual diagnostic algorithm. Do not transfer it to drug, contact or non-IgE allergy. [S691]

FDA's 2023 communication separately warns about false-negative food-allergen skin tests followed by serious reactions. This does not quantify the error rate of every blood or skin assay. A concerning reaction history is not permission for a home food challenge after a negative result. [S692]

**Original fictional example:** a person without a clear reaction history receives a panel with several positive results. Clarify the actual tests and relevant exposures. Do not turn a panel into an avoidance list, predict reaction severity or attempt an exposure experiment. Qualified clinical assessment may be needed to resolve the meaning.

**Reading and limits:** NLM-authored Medical Test page, updated 2024-11-19, relevant identity/result/limitations sections actually read; it is distinct from the third-party encyclopedia. FDA January 2025 definitions and selected food-evidence sections read by researcher; root checked source identity and relevant selected text. FDA 2023 issue/background/patient advice read. No cutoff, likelihood ratio, challenge protocol or complete current history-directed clinical guideline is supplied. ## AR2 — Symptom overlap and symptom relief are different questions

**Scope:** adult general education; selected England-facing rhinitis and US public-health sources.

Sneezing, nasal blockage or discharge, itchy eyes and cough can overlap between rhinitis and a cold. Exposure timing and triggers can help frame questions without proving an individual diagnosis. Nonallergic triggers and respiratory infections remain alternatives. [S21]

CDC distinguishes temporary symptom relief from curing or shortening a viral cold. Its adult outpatient summary says evidence for specific symptomatic therapies is limited and potential adverse effects matter. The consumer page describes rest, fluids, saline and age-qualified honey for comfort; honey is not for infants under one year. Antibiotics do not treat a viral cold. Increased risk and suspected flu/COVID-19 can make timely testing and clinician contact relevant. [S08, S09]

**Original fictional example:** seasonal congestion and cough do not by themselves establish allergy. Ask what exposure, timing, course, accompanying symptoms and risk would clarify the question. An OTC product offering comfort is not thereby shown to shorten illness. Breathing difficulty or other serious deterioration takes priority over this comparison.

**Reading and limits:** NHS rhinitis symptom/cause sections inspected; review date was not visible in that extraction. CDC consumer treatment page updated 2026-03-11 and adult outpatient summary dated 2024-04-16, relevant sections read. These sources do not provide a complete respiratory differential or contemporary comparative rhinitis-treatment review. No universal medicine choice or regimen follows. Earlier ARIA comparisons require separately verified usable source support.

## P1 — Young infants need age-specific assessment; missing fever is not reassurance

**Scope:** newborn/under-five signposting, not a diagnostic score. England-facing emergency routes must not be transported to another country.

A very young baby with markedly reduced feeding and very fast or difficult breathing needs immediate assessment through the local emergency service. Do not wait for every warning sign or for a measured fever. Severe breathing difficulty, a baby who cannot be woken, seizure or rapid deterioration warrants immediate local emergency help. The inspected England page also treats age-qualified fever as a serious sign, including 38 degrees C or higher under three months. The threshold is not a universal rule for every age, setting or measurement method. [S565]

**Original fictional example:** a 12-day-old feeds much less and is breathing faster; no temperature is available. Direct immediate assessment through the local emergency service for very fast/difficult breathing, without diagnosing or delaying to complete a diary. Report exact age, onset, feeding, breathing effort, responsiveness, wet nappies and any temperature/method already available.

**Reading and limits:** NHS urgent-under-five page, reviewed 2026-08-06: immediate-action, age-qualified fever, breathing, responsiveness and feeding/urine sections read by researcher and selected text rechecked by root. Its service routes concern England. This is not a comprehensive newborn examination or a universal fever/triage protocol. Prematurity, high-risk conditions and individual instructions need their own guidance. WHO 2022 full postnatal danger-sign recommendation remained inaccessible and is not reconstructed from an overview.

## P2 — Feeding observations and development checks support follow-up, not a diagnosis

**Scope:** selected infant-feeding and developmental surveillance concepts; keep global, England and US contexts distinct.

WHO's public feeding summary supports breastfeeding, complementary feeding around six months and skilled help for feeding difficulty. It is not an individual feeding plan. In the inspected NHS breastfeeding page, swallowing, age-related nappy output and weight trajectory are observations to discuss with skilled support. It describes at least six heavy wet nappies per 24 hours from day five; that number alone does not establish adequate intake or exclude illness. Seek help early for intake or growth concerns. [S689, S687]

England's health/development review page describes planned early contacts and the ability to seek advice between reviews. CDC distinguishes ongoing developmental monitoring from formal screening. It reports US/AAP developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months and autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months, with additional assessment when concerns arise. A missed checklist milestone does not by itself diagnose a disorder, and a scheduled screen is not a reason to defer a concern. [S688, S579]

**Original fictional example:** a six-day-old has few wet nappies and uncertain swallowing, with no weight follow-up. Recommend prompt feeding/weight assessment, without diagnosing dehydration or prescribing supplementation. Serious signs in P1 require emergency action. Separately, discuss a developmental concern with the child's clinician rather than treating a checklist as a verdict.

**Reading and limits:** WHO 2022 feeding-summary sections; NHS enough-milk page reviewed 2026-04-30 and selected early-review sections; CDC monitoring/screening page updated 2026-02-16 and reviewed 2026-02-17. Researcher read selected relevant sections; root rechecked breastfeeding and CDC screening text. No proprietary checklist was reproduced. Premature/high-risk infants, individualized lactation care, vaccination schedules and full developmental assessment remain separate questions.
